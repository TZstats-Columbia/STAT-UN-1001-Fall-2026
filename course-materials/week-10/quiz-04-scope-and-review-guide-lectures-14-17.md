## What you should be ready to do

### 1. Describe a statistic and its sampling distribution

You should be able to:

- identify a population parameter, a sample statistic, and the chance process that makes a statistic vary;
- explain why `x-bar` and `p-hat` are random variables before a sample is selected;
- distinguish the spread of individual values from the standard error, which describes typical sample-to-sample movement of a statistic;
- explain why a larger random sample generally reduces standard error while leaving a population parameter fixed; and
- distinguish improved precision from reduced bias.

### 2. Build and interpret a confidence interval for one proportion

You should be able to:

- identify the population, parameter `p`, sample, and point estimate `p-hat` in a study;
- check the conditions for a one-proportion confidence interval: a defensible sampling process, approximate independence, and at least 10 expected or observed successes and failures as used in class;
- use `SE = sqrt[p-hat(1 - p-hat) / n]`, `margin of error = z* x SE`, and `confidence interval = p-hat +/- margin of error` when the needed quantities are supplied or easy to compute;
- predict how confidence level, standard error, and sample size affect interval width;
- interpret an interval as a plausible range for the population proportion in context; and
- explain the repeated-sampling meaning of 95% confidence without treating the fixed population parameter as random.

### 3. Set up and interpret a one-proportion hypothesis test

You should be able to:

- define the population parameter and state `H0` and `HA` in context;
- use the two-sided alternative `HA: p does not equal to p0` for Quiz 4, explain why it looks for evidence in either direction, and distinguish it from a one-sided claim discussed in class;
- check one-proportion conditions under the null model;
- use the null-model standard error `SE0 = sqrt[p0(1 - p0) / n]` and test statistic `z = (p-hat - p0) / SE0` when the needed quantities are supplied or easy to compute;
- interpret a test statistic as the observed statistic's distance from the null-model expectation in standard-error units;
- interpret a p-value as the probability of results at least as extreme as those observed **if the null model were true**;
- use a stated significance level to decide whether to reject `H0` or fail to reject `H0`; and
- write a cautious conclusion in context without claiming to prove that `H0` or `HA` is true.

### 4. Evaluate an inferential claim carefully

You should be able to:

- distinguish a statistically significant result from a practically important result;
- explain why a large sample can detect a small difference;
- identify Type I and Type II errors by first naming the null hypothesis and then comparing the decision with reality;
- explain the connection between a 95% confidence interval and a two-sided test at the 5% significance level when the methods and conditions match; and
- state what an interval or test does **not** fix: biased sampling, weak measurement, an outdated target population, or a causal claim unsupported by the design.

## Key distinctions to keep straight

| Do not confuse | With |
| --- | --- |
| The population proportion `p` | The observed sample proportion `p-hat` |
| Standard error | Margin of error |
| The confidence multiplier `z*` | The hypothesis-test statistic `z` |
| The standard error based on `p-hat` for a confidence interval | The standard error based on `p0` for a hypothesis test |
| A p-value | The probability that `H0` is true |
| Failing to reject `H0` | Proving `H0` true |
| Statistical significance | Practical importance, good measurement, representativeness, or causation |

## Quiz boundaries

Quiz 4 will **not** ask you to:

- derive a sampling-distribution formula or prove a theorem;
- perform a long calculation with awkward arithmetic when a provided value can isolate the statistical reasoning;
- use a two-proportion procedure, a chi-square test, or any later Chapter 6 method;
- use a t distribution, build an interval for a population mean, or conduct a mean-based hypothesis test;
- memorize practice-quiz stories, article details, or a formula that you can reasonably place on your note sheet; or
- claim that a statistically significant result establishes a large practical effect, causation, or an unbiased study.

## Suggested review routine

1. For one sample-mean or sample-proportion scenario, identify the population parameter, chance process, statistic, sampling distribution, and standard error.
2. Work one confidence-interval problem from conditions through a complete interpretation in context.
3. Compare a 95% and a 99% confidence interval from the same sample and explain why the wider interval is wider.
4. Work one hypothesis-test problem in this order: hypotheses, conditions, null standard error, test statistic, p-value, decision, conclusion.
5. Practice writing the two-sided hypotheses for a research question and identifying the two tails that count as evidence against the null model.
6. For one small p-value, state both what it supports and what it does not establish about practical importance, bias, causation, or data quality.
7. Use the Week 08 and Week 09 practice quizzes to identify any step that still needs review before class.
