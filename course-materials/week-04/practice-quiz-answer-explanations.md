# Week 04 Practice Quiz Answer Explanations

These explanations are intended for review after you have completed the Week 04 practice quiz and after the week's class meetings. The Week 04 probability-preview questions are for practice and are not part of Quiz 1.

Source quiz: Flipped Activity Week 04: Practice Quiz Ch2-3 Categorical Data and Probability Preview

## Question 1. One-variable frequency table

Fourteen students report their usual way to get to campus:


subway, walk, subway, bus, subway, bike, walk, subway, bus, walk, subway, subway, bike, walk

Which frequency table is correct?

Correct answer:
subway: 6, walk: 4, bus: 2, bike: 2

Why:
Count each category once per student: subway appears 6 times, walk 4 times, bus 2 times, and bike 2 times. Step-by-step counting like this is practice support; on a quiz you would need to organize the counts yourself.

## Question 2. Relative frequency

Fourteen students report their usual way to get to campus:


subway, walk, subway, bus, subway, bike, walk, subway, bus, walk, subway, subway, bike, walk

What is the relative frequency for students who usually take the subway?

Correct answer:
6/14 = 0.429, about 43%

Why:
Relative frequency is count divided by total. There are 6 subway responses out of 14 students, so 6/14 = 0.429.

## Question 6. Exercise 2.22-style association

A small class activity compares two ways of getting help: asking a peer first or asking the instructor first. Among 16 students who asked a peer first, 10 said the explanation was helpful. Among 14 students who asked the instructor first, 11 said the explanation was helpful. Which statement is best supported?

Correct answer:
The helpfulness proportions are 10/16 = 62.5% and 11/14 = 78.6%; the instructor-first group was higher in this small sample, but this does not prove a causal effect.

Why:
Compare conditional proportions within each group, not raw group sizes. The observed difference is descriptive; unless the help method was randomly assigned and the design supports it, avoid causal language.

## Question 7. Exercise 3.1-style coin streak

A fair coin lands heads eight times in a row. What is the probability that the next toss lands heads?

Correct answer:
0.50; previous fair coin tosses do not change the probability of the next toss.

Why:
For a fair coin, each toss is modeled as independent. A streak may feel meaningful, but it does not change the next-toss probability in this model.

## Question 8. Exercise 3.1-style disjoint events

From a standard deck of cards, are the events 'draw a red card' and 'draw a queen' disjoint?

Correct answer:
No. A card can be both red and a queen, such as the queen of hearts.

Why:
Disjoint means the events cannot happen together. The queen of hearts and queen of diamonds are both red queens, so these events overlap.

## Question 9. Guided Practice 3.7-style disjoint addition

At a lunch counter, each customer chooses exactly one entree: salad, noodles, soup, or rice bowl. The probabilities are salad 0.20, noodles 0.35, soup 0.15, rice bowl 0.30. What is P(salad or rice bowl)?

Correct answer:
0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50

Why:
Because each customer chooses exactly one entree, salad and rice bowl are disjoint. For disjoint events, add the probabilities. These steps are included for practice; they will not be listed on a quiz.

## Question 10. Overlapping events are not disjoint

In the same class, let A = uses an iPhone and B = uses an Apple computer. Can we automatically use P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)?

Correct answer:
No. A student can be in both groups, so the overlap would be double-counted.

Why:
If events overlap, adding P(A) and P(B) counts the overlap twice. Use P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B).

## Question 11. Guided Practice 3.14-style Venn diagram

In the Venn diagram, how many students use both an iPhone and an Apple computer?

Correct answer:
22

Why:
The overlap of the two circles represents students who are in both events. The overlap is labeled 22.

## Question 12. Exercise 3.7-style union

What is the probability that a randomly selected student uses an iPhone or an Apple computer?

Correct answer:
(32 + 22 + 10)/100 = 0.64

Why:
The word 'or' means in iPhone only, both, or Apple-computer only. Add the three disjoint regions inside the circles: 32 + 22 + 10 = 64.

## Question 13. Exercise 3.7-style neither

What is the probability that a randomly selected student uses neither an iPhone nor an Apple computer?

Correct answer:
36/100 = 0.36

Why:
The region outside both circles is labeled neither. There are 36 such students out of 100.

## Question 14. Exercise 3.7-style independence check

Using the Venn diagram, are iPhone use and Apple-computer use independent?

Correct answer:
No. P(iPhone and Apple computer) = 0.22, while P(iPhone)P(Apple computer) = 0.54 x 0.32 = 0.1728.

Why:
One independence check is P(A and B) = P(A)P(B). Here 0.22 is not equal to 0.1728, so the events are not independent in this class survey.

## Question 15. Probability rules check

Which proposed probability distribution for preferred study location is valid?

Correct answer:
library 0.40, dorm 0.25, cafe 0.20, classroom 0.15

Why:
A probability distribution must have no negative probabilities and must sum to 1. Only the first option satisfies both rules.

## Question 16. Guided Practice 3.20-style notification probabilities

Number of phone notifications during one focused study hour
  Notifications01234 or more
  Probability0.180.250.220.160.19
What is P(no more than 3 notifications during the hour)?

Correct answer:
0.18 + 0.25 + 0.22 + 0.16 = 0.81

Why:
No more than 3 means 0, 1, 2, or 3 notifications. Add those probabilities. A complement shortcut also works: 1 - P(4 or more) = 1 - 0.19 = 0.81.

## Question 17. Independence with rain in two cities

For practice, suppose P(New York rain) = 0.30, P(Boston rain) = 0.35, and P(New York rain and Boston rain) = 0.18 on the same day. These are hypothetical numbers, not real weather data. Are the two rain events independent under this model?

Correct answer:
No. If independent, the joint probability would be 0.30 x 0.35 = 0.105, not 0.18.

Why:
For independence, compare the observed joint probability with the product of the marginal probabilities. This example is intentionally hypothetical; later we can ask whether real weather data support or challenge an independence assumption.

## Question 18. Exercise 3.11-style independence assumption

Suppose 48% of students in one section have taken a statistics course before, and 35% of students in another section have used spreadsheet formulas before. If we independently select one student from each section, what is the probability that the first has taken statistics before and the second has used spreadsheet formulas before?

Correct answer:
0.48 x 0.35 = 0.168

Why:
The word independently is the key assumption. Under independence, multiply the probabilities of the two events. In real settings, always ask whether independence is reasonable.
