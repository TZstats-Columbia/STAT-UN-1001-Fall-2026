## What you should be ready to do

### One-sample inference for a numerical outcome

- Recognize when a question concerns a numerical outcome and a population mean `mu`, rather than a categorical outcome and a population proportion `p`.
- Identify the population, sample, parameter, and statistic in a one-mean setting.
- Explain in plain language why the sample mean has sampling variability and why its estimated standard error is based on the sample standard deviation and sample size.
- Recognize the role of the `t` distribution when the population standard deviation is unknown; you will not need to derive a formula or use a long table lookup.
- Interpret a one-sample confidence interval for a population mean in context.
- Set up or interpret a one-sample hypothesis test for a population mean, including a null hypothesis, alternative hypothesis, p-value, and cautious conclusion.
- Check whether a method is plausible by attending to independence, sample size, unusual observations, skew, and the study design.

### Method choice and project reasoning

- Decide whether a project-style question calls for a one-proportion or one-mean procedure.
- Explain what a result supports, what it does not support, and which limitations matter.
- Communicate uncertainty, practical importance, data-quality concerns, and the scope of the claim.
- Distinguish a precise estimate from an unbiased or causally interpretable estimate.
