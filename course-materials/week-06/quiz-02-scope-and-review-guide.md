# Quiz 2 Scope and Review Guide

Quiz 2 will focus on OpenIntro Statistics Chapter 3 and Lectures 6-9. The central goal is probability reasoning: describing a chance process, identifying outcomes and events, choosing the right probability idea, and explaining what a probability statement means in context.

The quiz is not designed to reward fast calculation. Most questions will ask you to set up the situation carefully, choose a rule or representation, and interpret the result. Tables, Venn diagrams, and probability trees may appear because they help organize probability information.

Quiz 2 is planned for the final 15 minutes of class on Thursday, October 15, 2026. You may bring one single-sided page of notes.

## Main scope: what you should be able to do

### 1. Describe a chance process

You should be able to identify:

- what random action or process is being observed or repeated;
- the possible outcomes;
- the sample space in simple settings;
- the event of interest in words before computing;
- whether the outcome is categorical or numerical.

For example: randomly choosing a household, recording whether it has a cat, a car, or young children; drawing names from a small group; or observing whether it rains in two cities.

### 2. Use event language accurately

You should be able to decide whether events are:

- disjoint;
- overlapping;
- complements;
- independent;
- not independent.

You should also be able to use “or” carefully. If two events overlap, do not double-count the overlap. You should recognize complement pairs such as “at least one” and “none.”

### 3. Use probability representations

You should be able to read and use:

- Venn diagrams;
- two-way tables;
- simple probability tables;
- probability trees.

You do not need to memorize a special method for each representation. The point is to use the representation to see the event, the denominator, and the probability rule.

### 4. Compute simple probabilities

You should be able to:

- compute probabilities from equally likely outcomes when the setup is clear;
- use addition for disjoint events;
- use the general rule `P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)` when events overlap;
- use complements when they make the problem easier;
- multiply probabilities across stages when the setup justifies it;
- explain the result in the original context.

### 5. Distinguish joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities

You should be able to identify and interpret:

- marginal probability: one event or one variable, often found from a row total, column total, or overall total;
- joint probability: two events happening together, often found from one cell in a table;
- conditional probability: probability within a restricted group, often signaled by “given that,” “among,” or “if we know.”

For conditional probability, the most important question is often: **What is the denominator?**

### 6. Reason about independence

You should be able to explain independence in words: knowing one event happened does not change the probability of the other event.

You should also be able to:

- check independence using a table, Venn diagram, or probability statement;
- explain why disjoint events are usually not independent unless one event has probability 0;
- recognize when independence is assumed versus supported by information;
- connect independence to sampling with replacement.

### 7. Compare sampling with and without replacement

You should be able to explain:

- with replacement: the population resets after each draw, so repeated draws are independent in the usual setup;
- without replacement: the population changes after each draw, so probabilities can change;
- why sampling without replacement from a very large population can sometimes behave almost like independent sampling.

You may be asked to compute simple two- or three-stage probabilities when the setup is clear.

## Boundaries: what Quiz 2 will not emphasize

You are not expected to:

- Compute probabilities involving more than three stages or more than three outcomes.
- Memorize complicated formulas.
- Use random variables or probability distributions as formal objects.
- Use Bayes's rule or compute a reversed conditional probability as the correct-answer target.
- Compute expected value from a probability table.
- Use linear combinations of random variables.
- Compute variance or standard deviation from a probability table.
- Interpret continuous density curves.
- Use Chapter 4 named distribution models as formal models. Normal and binomial distributions are previewed in Week 06, but Quiz 2 focuses on Chapter 3 probability reasoning.
- Remember exact numbers or story details from practice quizzes.
- Reproduce long textbook examples from memory.

## Suggested review routine

1. Rework the Week 04 and Week 05 practice quizzes without looking at the answer explanations first.
2. Read the explanation pages for Week 04 and Week 05 after you have made a genuine attempt.
3. For each missed question, write one sentence naming the concept: disjoint, overlap, complement, independence, conditional probability, replacement, probability tree, Venn diagram, or denominator.
4. Practice translating probability notation into words and words into notation.
5. For any table problem, label the row totals, column totals, cell probabilities, and the denominator before calculating.
6. For any multi-stage problem, ask whether the process uses replacement or no replacement.
7. For any “at least one” problem, try the complement first.
8. For any probability table, ask whether probabilities add to 1 and where most of the probability is located.

## Final self-check

Before Quiz 2, you should be able to answer these questions in your own words:

- What is the chance process?
- What are the possible outcomes?
- What event am I trying to find the probability of?
- Are the events disjoint, overlapping, complements, or independent?
- Is this asking for a joint, marginal, or conditional probability?
- What is the denominator?
- Does replacement happen?
- Would a table, Venn diagram, or probability tree make the setup clearer?
