# Mini-project 1: Data Biography and Retrieval Audit

## Purpose

Investigate where retrieved data came from, what they measure, what they omit, and what claims they can support before beginning analysis.

## In-Class Work

1. Continue with the data from Mini-project 0 or retrieve a more suitable dataset using the [Data Search and Retrieval Assistant](https://pingpong.hks.harvard.edu/group/735?assistant=3600).
2. Identify the original data producer and source—not merely the assistant that helped retrieve it.
3. Document how observations were generated or collected.
4. Define the cases and each variable you expect to use, including units and whether variables are measured, counted, classified, or constructed.
5. Record geographic coverage, temporal coverage, aggregation level, missing values, filters, and exclusions.
6. Explain who or what is represented and what may be missing.
7. Write one claim the data could support and one claim they could not support.

## Submit

Submit a one-page data biography containing:

- Statistical question.
- Original data source and producer.
- Collection or generation process.
- Cases, variables, definitions, and units.
- Geographic, temporal, and aggregation coverage.
- Missingness, exclusions, and representation concerns.
- One supportable claim and one unsupported or overreaching claim.

Do not submit prompts, screenshots, or assistant conversation histories.

## Peer review

Canvas will automatically assign each student one anonymous peer review just after the submission deadline. Peer review is part of learning how to evaluate statistical work constructively.

Your peer feedback should be **constructive and curious**. Focus on helping the author strengthen the work, not on judging the person. Useful peer review often includes:

- One thing that is clear, interesting, or promising.
- One question you genuinely have about the data source, variables, coverage, or claim.
- One specific suggestion that would make the data biography more precise or more convincing.

Good feedback can ask questions such as: What does one row represent? Who produced the data? What place and time period are covered? What might be missing? Which claim is supported by the data, and which claim would overreach?

## Grading rubric

This assignment is graded generously, but not for completion alone. Strong submissions explain why the problem matters, identify the original data source, describe the data clearly, and avoid overclaiming what the data can show.

Suggested total: 10 points. Half-points are appropriate.

### 1. Originality and importance of the problem: 1.5 points

- 1.5: Specific, interesting problem with a clear reason it matters.
- 1.0: Reasonable problem, but importance or originality is only partly explained.
- 0.5: Problem is present but generic, vague, or weakly motivated.
- 0: No meaningful statistical problem or motivation.

### 2. Original data source and provenance: 1.5 points

- 1.5: Original producer/source is specific and traceable.
- 1.0: Source is plausible but missing some provenance detail.
- 0.5: Mentions a general source, but the original dataset is hard to identify.
- 0: No identifiable original data source.

### 3. Collection process, cases, variables, and units: 2.5 points

- 2.5: Collection process, cases, variables, definitions, and units are clear and mostly correct.
- 2.0: Main elements are clear, with minor type, definition, or unit issues.
- 1.5: Understandable but with some confusion about cases, aggregation, definitions, or units.
- 0.75: Some variables are listed, but cases or collection process are substantially unclear.
- 0: No meaningful description of how the data were generated or what the variables mean.

### 4. Coverage, aggregation, missingness, and exclusions: 1.5 points

- 1.5: Coverage, aggregation, missingness, and exclusions are specific and matched to the data.
- 1.0: Most elements are present, but one or two are vague or incomplete.
- 0.5: Mentions coverage or missingness only generally.
- 0: Coverage, aggregation, missingness, and exclusions are missing or not interpretable.

### 5. Supportable claim and important limitation: 2 points

- 2.0: Supportable and unsupported claims are clear; limitation is specific and statistically meaningful.
- 1.5: Claims and limitation are present, with minor overstatement or vagueness.
- 1.0: Only one claim is clear, or the limitation is generic but relevant.
- 0.5: Claims or limitation are very vague or weakly connected to the data.
- 0: No meaningful limitation, or claims seriously overreach the data.

### 6. Clarity and organization: 1 point

- 1.0: Easy to follow and complete enough to grade reliably.
- 0.75: Understandable with minor organization issues.
- 0.5: Important pieces are present but hard to locate or repetitive.
- 0: Too unclear to grade reliably.

## Completion Criteria

Full completion credit requires clear evidence that you checked provenance, definitions, coverage, and limitations rather than treating the retrieved result as self-explanatory. Assistant use itself is not graded.
