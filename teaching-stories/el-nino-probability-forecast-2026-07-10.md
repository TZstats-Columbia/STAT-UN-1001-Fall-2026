## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story. It gives students an accessible entry into probabilistic forecasting and explains the main percentages without requiring technical climate knowledge.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

On July 10, 2026, news coverage of NOAA forecasts said there was an 81% chance that the developing El Nino would become "very strong" by late 2026 and a 97% chance it would persist into spring 2027. This is a useful statistics story because it is about probability, uncertainty, and how forecasts are communicated to the public. Students can ask what those percentages mean, what event is being predicted, how a forecast differs from a guarantee, and why even a high-probability forecast still leaves room for different outcomes.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it centers on forecast probabilities rather than observed outcomes. Students often read a number like 81% as if the event is basically certain, but introductory statistics should slow that down. A forecasted probability describes uncertainty before the event occurs. It is not the same as a claim that the event already happened or definitely will happen.

The story also supports careful work on operational definitions. "Very strong El Nino" is not casual language; it refers to a threshold category. Students can discuss how changing the threshold changes the story, how forecast windows matter, and why one forecast can be both highly informative and still uncertain. The story also helps distinguish long-term climate trends from short-term event probabilities, which is valuable for class discussion about public claims and scientific caution.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Translate The Forecast

Ask students to rewrite "81% chance of a very strong El Nino" in plain language that avoids sounding certain.

### Define The Event

Have students state exactly what event the 81% refers to and what event the 97% refers to. Then compare how the two events differ.

### Threshold Thinking

Discuss why the phrase "very strong" must depend on a numeric cutoff. Ask students how a slightly different cutoff could change the reported probability.

### Forecast Versus Outcome

Ask students what evidence would be needed in early 2027 to evaluate whether the forecast performed well.

## Discussion Questions

1. What does the 81% probability actually refer to?
2. Why is a forecast probability not the same as a guarantee?
3. How is "persist into spring 2027" a different event from "become very strong by late 2026"?
4. Why might news readers overinterpret a high probability?
5. How could a forecast be reasonable even if the predicted event does not occur?
6. What additional information would help you judge whether this forecasting system is trustworthy?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: probability interpretation.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: What does an 81% chance of a very strong El Nino mean?
- Answer: It means forecasters estimate that the event has a high probability of occurring, not that it is guaranteed.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: event definition.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: Are "becoming very strong by late 2026" and "persisting into spring 2027" the same event?
- Answer: No. They are different forecast events with different time windows and conditions.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: uncertainty language.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: rewrite.
- Prompt: Rewrite the headline claim in one sentence that keeps the uncertainty visible.
- Answer: A strong answer says that forecasters estimate a high chance of a very strong El Nino, but the outcome is not certain.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: threshold categories.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Why does the phrase "very strong" matter statistically?
- Answer: Because it depends on a cutoff or category definition, and the reported probability changes depending on how that event is defined.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: forecast evaluation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: design.
- Prompt: What information would you need to evaluate whether this forecast was well calibrated?
- Answer: A strong answer mentions past forecasts and outcomes, or many similar forecasts over time, so predicted probabilities can be compared with what actually happened.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: caution with causal and predictive claims.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A student says, "Since the forecast says 81%, we know the event will happen." What is a better response?
- Answer: An 81% forecast indicates a high chance, but there is still meaningful uncertainty, so the event is not known with certainty in advance.

## Teaching Note

This story is especially useful early in the course because forecast probabilities are familiar from weather coverage, but students often misread them. It creates a natural bridge from everyday language into formal reasoning about uncertainty.
