## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press coverage from July 14 and July 17, 2026. The AP stories are readable, concrete, and easy for students to follow from an early suspicion about lettuce to a later source confirmation tied to Taco Bell locations in five states.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

In July 2026, public-health officials traced part of a large Cyclospora outbreak to shredded iceberg lettuce served at Taco Bell locations in five states. This is a useful statistics story because the numbers are messy in realistic ways: some cases were confirmed, many more were still under review, reports came from more than 30 states, and not every illness was believed to have the same source. Students can use the story to ask how outbreaks are counted, why official totals lag, how interviews with patients help identify a likely source, and why a large number of illnesses still does not automatically prove one simple explanation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because it shows how real data arrive in stages. On July 14, 2026, the AP reported that more than 30 states had reported infections, that state-level totals already exceeded the previous 2019 record of about 4,700 cases, and that officials had not yet definitively identified the source. By July 17, 2026, the AP reported that federal officials had linked a subset of the outbreak to shredded iceberg lettuce served at Taco Bell locations in Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia.

That sequence gives students a clean way to discuss undercounting, delayed reporting, case definitions, and why subsets matter. FDA reported 1,644 people in the five-state Taco Bell-linked subset, while AP also described many other reported infections nationwide that may not all belong to the same outbreak. Students can examine why different totals can all be correct when they refer to different populations, different dates, or different levels of confirmation.

The story also supports cautious causal reasoning. Interviews with patients, traceback work, and timing patterns all matter, but they do not erase uncertainty about other exposures, travel-related cases, or infections that were never diagnosed. This is a strong introductory example of how evidence accumulates without becoming magically complete.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### One Story, Several Denominators

Ask students to list at least three different groups counted in the coverage: all reported U.S. cases, confirmed domestic cases, the five-state Taco Bell-linked subset, and a single state total such as Michigan.

### Reconstruct The Timeline

Give students short excerpts from the July 13, July 14, July 16, and July 17 sources. Have them map how the claim changed from "possible source" to "linked subset" to "confirmed source for a five-state subset."

### Why Totals Lag

Ask students why CDC totals can be lower than state tallies in the short run. Then discuss symptom delay, lab confirmation, and data review.

### Interview Data And Bias

Use the Michigan patient interviews as an example of observational evidence. Ask what could go wrong when people are asked to remember what they ate.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why can two credible sources report different totals for the same broad outbreak period?
2. What is the difference between a nationwide surge and a five-state linked subset?
3. Why might the true number of infections be larger than the official number?
4. How do patient interviews help investigators narrow down a likely food source?
5. Why is "many sick people ate lettuce" not, by itself, enough to prove lettuce caused every case?
6. What additional data would make you more confident about the source of infections outside the five Taco Bell-linked states?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain how the reported number of Cyclospora cases can depend on whether officials count suspected cases, confirmed cases, or only cases tied to a specific outbreak subset.

### Concept Check

Which is the better description of 1,644: all U.S. Cyclospora cases in 2026, or the five-state Taco Bell-linked subset reported by FDA?

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason the true number of infections can be higher than the reported number.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: subset versus total.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: FDA reports 1,644 people in a five-state Taco Bell-linked outbreak subset. Does that number represent all U.S. Cyclospora cases in 2026?
- Answer: No. It refers only to a subset of cases linked to a specific five-state outbreak investigation.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: reporting lag.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Why might state case counts appear before lower federal totals?
- Answer: States may post reports faster, while federal totals require additional review and classification.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: undercounting.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Give one reason the true number of infected people may be higher than the reported number.
- Answer: Some people may have mild illness, never get tested, or never receive a confirmed diagnosis.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: recall bias.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Investigators interviewed patients about what they ate. What is one limitation of that evidence?
- Answer: People may forget, misremember, or overreport foods they heard about in the news.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: cautious causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A student says, "Since many patients ate lettuce, lettuce caused every case." What is a better response?
- Answer: Lettuce may explain some cases, but other exposures, travel, and unrelated infections mean the same cause may not explain every reported case.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: comparing numbers from different populations.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why can "more than 30 states reported infections" and "the source was confirmed for five states" both be true at the same time?
- Answer: Because infections were reported broadly nationwide, but the confirmed source attribution referred to a narrower five-state outbreak subset.

## Teaching Note

This story works especially well if students have already seen simple bar charts or tables. It gives a realistic reason to ask, "Count of what?" before interpreting any headline number.
