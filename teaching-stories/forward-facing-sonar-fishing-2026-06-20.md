# Forward-Facing Sonar and Fishing - 2026-06-20

## Original Source Links

- AP News: [Some anglers praise forward-facing sonar, others say high-tech fishing ruins a day at the lake](https://apnews.com/article/fishing-sonar-anglers-boating-f3a7b84b0a04f997bf4f043c9f124276), June 15, 2026.

## Accessible Student Source

Use the AP News article. It is accessible, concrete, and easy to discuss even for students who know little about fishing.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

New sonar devices let anglers see fish and even their lures in real time. Some people think this technology must lead to catching many more fish, while others say that belief is based more on perception than evidence. The AP article reports survey results showing many anglers now use the technology on some lakes, but it also describes studies in Wisconsin and Minnesota that did not find a big increase in catch rates. This is a useful statistics story because it compares anecdotes with data, asks what outcome should be measured, and shows why a strong opinion is not the same as strong evidence.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is about a practical question that students can understand immediately: does better technology actually change results? The AP article reports that surveys in Minnesota found about 30% of anglers use forward-facing sonar, with usage as high as 63% on one lake. It also reports a controlled Wisconsin experiment in which anglers with and without the technology were compared and then switched halfway through the summer. The reported result was surprising: the group without sonar had higher catch rates, while the sonar group caught slightly longer fish.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it naturally raises questions about outcome variables, comparison, variability, and external validity. Students can see the difference between "catching more fish," "catching bigger fish," "fishing more efficiently," and "damaging fish populations." It is also a clean way to discuss why one study on one species or set of lakes may not generalize everywhere.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 2: Compare anecdote, survey evidence, and experimental evidence.
- Week 3: Work with percentages, proportions, and group comparisons.
- Week 4: Discuss variability and limits of generalization.
- Week 5: Use the story to discuss conditional statements such as catch success given sonar use versus without sonar.
- Week 6: Treat catch count or fish length as random variables that vary from trip to trip even with the same technology.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: anglers, fishing trips, lakes, or fish caught.
- Variables: sonar use, catch count, fish length, lake, species, time spent searching, time spent fishing.
- Survey proportion.
- Controlled comparison.
- Outcome choice and operational definition.
- External validity.
- Perception versus measured evidence.
- Confounding when technology users differ from other anglers.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define "Success"

Ask students to list at least four possible response variables for the question "Does sonar improve fishing?" Then have them compare how conclusions could change depending on which variable is chosen.

### Compare Evidence Types

Put three forms of evidence on the board: personal anecdotes, angler surveys, and the Wisconsin comparison study. Students rank which is strongest for which kind of claim and explain why.

### Percent Interpretation

Use the reported Minnesota survey figures of about 30% statewide use and as high as 63% on one lake. Ask students what those numbers do and do not imply about all anglers.

### Generalization Check

Have students explain why a result from smallmouth bass in Wisconsin might not automatically apply to walleye, muskie, or every lake.

## Discussion Questions

1. What exactly is the claim people are debating in this story?
2. What should count as a case in a dataset about this question?
3. Why is "catch rate" not the only reasonable outcome variable?
4. What makes the Wisconsin comparison more informative than an anecdote from one angler?
5. Why might technology change fish size caught without changing the total number caught?
6. What additional data would you want before making a policy recommendation?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The AP article reports that about 30% of anglers in Minnesota use forward-facing sonar, with one surveyed lake reaching 63%. Explain why those numbers should not automatically be treated as the same thing.

### Study Design Prompt

Design a simple study to investigate whether forward-facing sonar increases catch rates. Identify the cases, explanatory variable, response variable, and one possible confounder or limitation.

### Exit Ticket

Write one sentence explaining the difference between "many anglers believe sonar helps" and "data show sonar increases catch rates."

### Mini-Rubric

- Clearly identifies cases and variables.
- Distinguishes survey evidence from experimental evidence.
- Uses percentages carefully.
- Notes at least one limit on generalization.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: outcome variable choice.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: If the question is whether sonar helps anglers catch bigger fish, what is the response variable?
- Answer: Fish size or length.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: evidence types.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: A few anglers saying sonar helps is stronger evidence than a controlled comparison study.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: If 30% of anglers statewide use sonar and one lake has 63% use, how many percentage points higher is the lake's use rate than the statewide rate?
- Answer: 63% - 30% = 33 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting mixed outcomes.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: The study found sonar users did not catch more fish on average but did catch slightly longer fish. Write one careful sentence summarizing that result.
- Answer: A strong answer says sonar did not clearly increase catch count in that study, though it may have been associated with somewhat larger fish.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: external validity.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is a result from smallmouth bass in Wisconsin not automatically enough to settle the question for all anglers everywhere?
- Answer: Species, lakes, fishing conditions, and user behavior differ, so the result may not generalize broadly.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: comparative study design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Design a stronger follow-up study of sonar. Specify the cases, one explanatory variable, two response variables, and one reason your design would improve on anecdotal evidence.
- Answer: A strong answer identifies comparable trips or anglers, sonar use as the explanatory variable, catch count and fish size as responses, and explains how structured comparison reduces bias.
