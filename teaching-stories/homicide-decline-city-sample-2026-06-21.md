Use the AP News article. It is accessible, avoids technical jargon, and clearly states both the headline decrease and the cautions about interpretation.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A report using data from 35 U.S. cities found that homicide rates fell by 21% from 2024 to 2025, which the article describes as about 922 fewer homicides in those cities. At the same time, the report warns that the cities are not a national sample, definitions can differ across places, and it is too early to say exactly why the decline happened. This is a strong statistics story because it shows how a dramatic number can still come with important limits about sampling, measurement, and causal interpretation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is built around a Council on Criminal Justice report using police and municipal data from large cities that consistently reported monthly figures. The report says homicide fell in 31 of 35 cities and that several other crime categories also declined, but it repeatedly warns that the cities are not necessarily representative of the entire United States and that local crime data can later be revised. It also notes that many crimes are never reported to police at all.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it gives students practice asking a core question: "What population does this evidence really describe?" It also illustrates why rates, definitions, and data-collection systems matter. Most importantly, it is an excellent case for cautious causal reasoning, since a large change in an outcome does not by itself identify which policy or social factor caused it.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Population Check

Ask students whether "35 cities" is enough to support a statement about "the United States." Then ask what additional information they would want before generalizing nationally.

### Counts Versus Rates

Give students two hypothetical cities with different populations and homicide counts. Ask them to explain why rates are more comparable than raw counts.

### Causal Language Revision

Provide a few headline-style statements such as "Policy X caused crime to fall" or "Cities got safer because of Y." Have students rewrite them using more defensible statistical language.

### Data Limitations Discussion

Ask students what happens to inference when some crimes are unreported, some cities use different definitions, and some totals are revised later.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?

3. Why are homicide rates usually more informative than raw homicide counts?
4. How do underreporting and different definitions affect interpretation?
5. Why is it risky to credit one policy or political leader for the decline?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The article reports a 21% drop in homicide rates across 35 cities. Explain what that number describes and give one reason it should not automatically be interpreted as a national causal result.

### Study Design Prompt

Suppose you want to study whether homicide trends in these cities represent national trends. What additional data or design features would help you make a stronger generalization?

### Exit Ticket

Why does the report's warning that the cities are "not necessarily representative" matter for interpreting the headline?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: sample versus population.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: The report summarizes 35 U.S. cities. In this context, what is the sample and what broader population might readers incorrectly generalize to?
- Answer: The sample is the 35 included cities. Readers might incorrectly generalize to all U.S. cities or the entire United States.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: rates versus counts.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If one city has more homicides than another, it must also have a higher homicide rate.
- Answer: False. A larger city can have more homicides but a lower rate.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: A city had a homicide rate of 10 per 100,000 in 2024 and 8 per 100,000 in 2025. What was the percent change?
- Answer: The change is -2 per 100,000. The percent change is -2/10 = -20%, so the rate fell by 20%.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpretation of evidence.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: The article reports that homicide fell in 31 of 35 cities. Write one sentence describing what that pattern shows and one sentence stating what it does not prove.
- Answer: A strong answer says the decline was widespread in this city sample. It does not prove the same pattern occurred nationwide or identify a single cause.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: data limitations.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why does underreporting matter less for homicide than for some other crimes, but still not disappear as a concern in crime data?
- Answer: Homicide is more likely to be recorded than many other crimes, but classification rules, revisions, and jurisdiction differences can still affect the data.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: stronger design for generalization.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Propose one way to strengthen a claim that the 2025 decline reflects a national trend rather than a pattern limited to the cities in the report.
- Answer: A strong answer proposes a more representative national sample or a comprehensive national dataset with consistent definitions and timing.
