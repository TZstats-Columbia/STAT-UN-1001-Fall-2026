## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story from September 1, 2026. It is vivid and easy for students to follow, but it also introduces subtle statistical questions about detection, observation technology, rare events, and the gap between more sightings and more actual sharks.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

Marine researchers off Long Island are catching, tagging, and releasing sharks to study where they travel and which species use local waters. The same story notes that many people feel like there are "more sharks than ever," but researchers suggest part of that impression comes from better detection through drones, phones, and tracking technology rather than a simple population surge. This is a useful statistics story because it helps students separate the thing being measured from the way it is observed, and it raises basic questions about rare events, proxies, and how new technology can change what trends look like.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it begins with a popular fear and then complicates it using evidence. Students can immediately understand the public claim: people think shark encounters are increasing. But the researchers' explanation is more careful. We may be seeing more because the animals are being observed more often, not necessarily because the underlying population has changed by the same amount. That is a classic measurement and detection issue.

The story also supports reasoning about rare events and misleading denominators. Shark bites are emotionally powerful, but they are rare compared with the number of swimmers, beach visits, or even non-bite shark sightings. Students can ask what denominator matters if they want to judge risk and why a few dramatic incidents can distort perception.

This example is also useful for discussing observational data in environmental science. Tags provide movement data, but the sample of tagged sharks is not the entire shark population. Students can ask how tagged animals are selected, whether some species are easier to detect, and what kinds of claims are justified from those data. That makes the story concrete without becoming technically advanced.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### More Sharks Or More Cameras?

Ask students to list at least three ways shark observations could increase even if the shark population stayed the same.

### Build The Right Denominator

Have students propose better denominators for public risk questions, such as bites per million beach visits instead of raw bite counts.

### Sightings Versus Risk

Students compare the meanings of "more sharks were seen" and "the danger to swimmers increased."

### Sample Limits

Students discuss what scientists can and cannot conclude from a limited set of tagged sharks.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why might shark sightings increase even if shark abundance does not increase by the same amount?
2. What is the difference between a sighting, a tagged shark, and a shark bite in statistical terms?
3. Why are rare but memorable events often hard to reason about well?
4. What denominator would you want before making a claim about beach safety?
5. How could improved technology create an apparent trend?
6. What limits should students keep in mind when interpreting tagging data?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain how better detection technology could make it seem like sharks are becoming more common even if the underlying population changed little.

### Concept Check

Which statement is best supported by the evidence?

- A. More shark sightings prove that shark populations doubled
- B. Tagging a few sharks reveals the exact size of the shark population
- C. Increased observation technology can change sighting counts without equally changing actual risk
- D. A rare shark bite means Long Island is unusually dangerous overall

Correct answer: C.

### Exit Ticket

Give one example of a denominator that would help interpret shark risk more fairly than a raw count of bites.

### Mini-Rubric

- Distinguishes observation from underlying population.
- Uses counts and rates appropriately.
- Mentions detection or selection bias.
- Avoids overgeneralizing from a small sample of tagged animals.
