## Student-Facing Paragraph

A September 2026 news story reported that a planned national EPA survey of PFAS in wastewater and sewage sludge had been stuck in federal review for much longer than usual. The proposed study would begin with about 400 wastewater treatment plants and then select a subset for sampling to help identify where these "forever chemicals" are entering the system. This is a useful statistics story because it asks students to think about sampling design, missing data, measurement, and why policy debates often hinge on whether the available data are representative and complete enough to support broad claims.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because the central issue is not advanced chemistry. It is basic statistical reasoning about how public evidence gets built. The story gives students a concrete example of a national claim that depends on a sampling plan: the EPA intended to collect questionnaire data from about 400 publicly owned treatment works and use a subset for more intensive sampling. That lets students talk about cases, variables, sampling frames, and why a subset may still be informative if it is selected thoughtfully.

The story also works well for missing-data reasoning. Officials, advocates, and industry groups disagree partly because the national dataset does not yet exist. Some argue there is already enough information from states and local studies, while others argue that those pieces are incomplete or inconsistent. That creates a clean classroom contrast between "some data exist" and "we have representative national evidence."

This is also a strong story for operational definitions and cautious language. Students can ask what exactly counts as PFAS contamination, what unit is being measured, which facilities enter the study, and whether findings about sampled treatment plants would generalize to all plants nationwide. The story encourages careful thinking without requiring technical background in environmental science.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Map The Study Design

Ask students to diagram the planned study from questionnaire stage to sampling stage and identify where bias could enter.

### National Or Not?

Have students discuss whether data from a few states are enough to support a national claim about PFAS discharges.

### Fix The Headline

Students revise a headline like "PFAS is everywhere in U.S. wastewater" into wording that better matches the evidence currently available.

### Missing Data Brainstorm

Students list reasons a treatment plant dataset might be incomplete even if many facilities are contacted.

## Discussion Questions

1. What are the cases and variables in the proposed EPA study?
2. Why does it matter that the study begins with a defined set of treatment plants?
3. What is the difference between having some state data and having a national dataset?
4. How could sampled facilities differ from facilities not sampled?
5. Why should students be cautious about broad claims when a study is still pending?
6. What additional information would help you judge whether the study design is representative?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why a planned sample of wastewater treatment plants could still produce useful national evidence, even though not every plant in the country would be measured.

### Concept Check

Which statement is best supported by the evidence?

- A. The United States already has complete national PFAS wastewater data
- B. The proposed EPA study was designed to collect broader national evidence than isolated local studies
- C. Every treatment plant in the country would be sampled directly
- D. The available evidence proves all industries contribute equally to PFAS contamination

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason a national dataset might lead to a different conclusion than a collection of local studies.
