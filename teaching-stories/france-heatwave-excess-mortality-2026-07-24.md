Use the AP News story as the main student-facing reading. It is short, concrete, and readable, while still pointing students toward the official public-health estimates behind the headline.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

French public-health officials said that during the heat wave from June 17 to July 2, 2026, there were at least 5,764 more deaths than expected in the affected parts of mainland France. The estimate compares the number of deaths that actually occurred with the number that would normally have been expected for that period. This is a strong statistics story because students can examine how a dramatic headline depends on a baseline, a model for "expected" deaths, and careful interpretation of what "excess deaths" does and does not mean.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is an excellent fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it makes a basic but important statistical move visible: comparing observed data with an expected reference value. Students can see that "5,764 excess deaths" is not a direct count of deaths labeled "caused by heat." It is a comparison between 21,674 observed all-cause deaths and 15,910 expected all-cause deaths during heat-wave days in the affected departments.

That distinction matters. It helps students practice separating raw counts from derived statistics and understanding why a baseline is essential for interpretation. It also opens a useful discussion of uncertainty. The French agency explicitly described the estimate as preliminary and based on unconsolidated data. Students can therefore ask how delayed reporting, model choice, and the definition of the heat-wave period affect the result.

The story also supports cautious causal reasoning. The excess-mortality estimate is strong evidence that the heat event had serious health consequences, but it is not the same thing as proving that every one of the excess deaths would be medically certified as heat-related. That makes this story especially good for teaching the limits and value of indirect statistical evidence.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Rebuild The Headline Number

Give students the observed total of 21,674 deaths and the expected total of 15,910 deaths. Ask them to compute the excess deaths and the percent increase, then explain in words what each number means.

### What Does "Expected" Mean?

Have students discuss how an "expected" number of deaths might be produced. This is a useful entry point to seasonality, long-term trend, and why analysts do not simply compare one week to the immediately previous week.

### Direct Count Or Statistical Estimate?

Ask students whether "5,764 excess deaths" is best understood as a direct count, a model-based estimate, or both. Then discuss why this distinction matters in public communication.

### Regional Comparison

Use the report's note that Ile-de-France had 1,999 excess deaths and ask students what additional information they would want before deciding whether that region was "worst affected" in a fair comparative sense.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the difference between observed deaths and excess deaths in this story?
2. Why is an expected baseline necessary before saying deaths were unusually high?
3. Why is "all-cause mortality" used here instead of only deaths officially labeled as heat-related?
4. What does it mean that the estimate is preliminary?
5. Why should students be careful not to interpret excess deaths as a perfect one-to-one count of deaths caused by heat?
6. What other graphs or tables would help you understand the pattern more fully?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why the statement "5,764 excess deaths" is not the same as saying exactly 5,764 death certificates listed heat as the cause of death.

### Concept Check

In this story, what two quantities are being compared to produce the excess-death estimate?

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason the published estimate might later be revised.

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies observed and expected counts.
- Explains excess deaths in context.
- Notes that the estimate is indirect and preliminary.
- Avoids overstating causation.

## Teaching Note

This story works especially well when students are first learning that meaningful comparison often requires a reference point. It is also a clean example of how official statistics can be powerful even when they are incomplete or model-based.
