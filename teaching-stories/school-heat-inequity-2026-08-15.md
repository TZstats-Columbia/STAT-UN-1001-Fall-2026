## Student-Facing Paragraph

A July 2026 article explained that many students are spending more school time in extreme heat, and that the burden is not spread evenly across schools. Research on U.S. public schools found that students from some racial and ethnic minority groups and students eligible for free or reduced-price lunch are more likely to attend schools with the highest frequency of extreme heat days. This is a useful statistics story because it combines percentages, thresholds, and group comparisons with a broader question: when a school is labeled "high heat," how was that measured, and what can those data say about inequality without oversimplifying causes?

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it turns a socially important claim into a set of measurable variables. The study linked school locations to temperature data and compared which students were overrepresented in the hottest schools. Students can identify the cases as schools, distinguish school-level and student-level variables, and ask how a threshold such as days above 90 degrees Fahrenheit changes the resulting pattern.

The topic is highly accessible. Students do not need technical climate science to understand school buildings, playgrounds, missing class, or needing air conditioning. The Washington Post / *The Conversation* article adds everyday quantities that invite interpretation, such as absence rates being 16% higher on days above 90 F, an estimate that 41% of districts with air conditioning need updates or replacement, and 156 districts serving more than 1 million students experiencing at least 30 more school-year days above 80 F in 2025 than in 1970.

The story also supports careful reasoning about inequality. The study reports disparities in exposure, but it does not mean student race or income mechanically causes higher temperatures. Students can discuss confounding through geography, regional climate, school infrastructure, neighborhood investment, tree cover, and policy decisions about school calendars and facilities.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 2: Define cases, variables, and levels of analysis.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define The Cases

Ask students whether the cases in the main study are students or schools, and what difference that makes for interpreting claims about inequality.

### Threshold Swap

Have students compare two definitions of an extreme heat day, such as above 90 F versus above a local 95th percentile, and discuss why group rankings might change when the measurement rule changes.

### Rewrite A Headline Carefully

Students revise a headline like "Minority students attend the hottest schools" into language that keeps the descriptive finding while avoiding an unsupported causal interpretation.

### Local Decision Memo

Students draft a short memo advising a school district what extra local data it would need before deciding whether to spend money on shade, cooling systems, or calendar changes.

## Discussion Questions

1. What are the cases in the school-heat study?
2. Why might a school-level result be misread as a claim about every individual student?
3. What is gained and lost by defining extreme heat with a fixed threshold like 90 F?
4. Why is overrepresentation in the hottest schools not the same as proof of a single cause?
5. What school or neighborhood factors might confound the relationship between student demographics and heat exposure?
6. What additional information would help a local district decide what intervention matters most?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: cases and variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: definition.
- Prompt: In the published school-heat study, what are the cases?
- Answer: The cases are public schools, each linked to temperature and demographic information.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: percentage interpretation.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: translation.
- Prompt: A news story says school absence rates are 16% higher on days above 90 F. What does that compare?
- Answer: It compares absence rates on very hot days with absence rates on cooler days.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: operational definition.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why might researchers examine both days above 90 F and days above a local 95th percentile?
- Answer: Because a fixed national threshold and a location-specific threshold capture different ideas of heat exposure and may reveal different patterns across regions.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: aggregate versus individual interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Why is it incorrect to conclude that every student in a hotter school has the same heat exposure?
- Answer: The study uses school-level data, so conditions can still vary within schools, across classrooms, and across student experiences.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Give one plausible confounder in the relationship between school demographics and extreme heat exposure.
- Answer: Region is one plausible confounder because climate differs substantially across states and cities and is also related to school demographics.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious inference.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: A classmate says, "This proves that changing school start dates will solve heat inequity." What is a better response?
- Answer: The data show unequal heat exposure and suggest several possible contributors, but they do not isolate whether changing calendars alone would solve the problem.
