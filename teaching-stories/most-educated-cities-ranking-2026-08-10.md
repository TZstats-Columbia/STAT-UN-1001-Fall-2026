## Student-Facing Paragraph

A 2026 ranking named Ann Arbor, Michigan the "most educated" metro area in the United States, followed by Durham-Chapel Hill, San Jose, Washington, DC, and Madison. The ranking is not based on one number. WalletHub compared the 150 largest metropolitan statistical areas using 11 metrics, including degree attainment, public-school quality, university quality, and education gaps by race and gender. This is a useful statistics story because it asks what a ranking really measures, how weights change a final score, and why a place can rank highly on one education measure but not another.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story focuses on a familiar kind of public data product: a ranked list of cities. WalletHub compared 150 large U.S. metropolitan statistical areas and assigned each a 0-100 total score. The score combines two broad dimensions: Educational Attainment, worth 80 points, and Quality of Education & Attainment Gap, worth 20 points. Ann Arbor ranked first with a score of 94.48, while Durham-Chapel Hill ranked second with 85.00. The large gap between first and second is itself a good invitation to ask whether ranks and scores tell the same story.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because rankings feel simple but depend on many statistical choices. Students can identify cases, variables, composite scores, weights, operational definitions, and comparison groups. They can also critique the meaning of "most educated": does it mean the largest share of adults with degrees, the strongest schools, the smallest education gaps, the best universities, or a weighted combination chosen by the ranking maker?

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: metropolitan statistical areas.
- Variables: high school diploma share, some-college share, bachelor's degree share, graduate degree share, public-school quality, university quality, racial education gap, gender education gap, and total score.
- Composite index construction.
- Weighting: educational attainment counts for 80% of the score; quality and gaps count for 20%.
- Rank versus score.
- Operational definition: what "educated city" means in this ranking.
- Data sources and derived variables.
- Comparability across places.
- Map and ranking communication.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### What Does "Most Educated" Mean?

Ask students to write their own definition of a "most educated city" before seeing the WalletHub methodology. Then compare student definitions with the actual metrics used in the ranking.

### Rank Versus Score

Give students the top five scores: Ann Arbor 94.48, Durham-Chapel Hill 85.00, San Jose 82.67, Washington, DC 81.93, and Madison 79.62. Ask them which pair of adjacent ranks is most different in score and why rank alone hides that information.

### Reweight The Index

Ask groups to propose alternative weights. For example, what if attainment counted for 50% and equality gaps counted for 50%? Students do not need to recompute the whole ranking; they should explain which kinds of cities might move up or down.

### Map Critique

Show the infographic map and ask what it communicates well and what it hides. Prompt students to notice that the visual emphasizes the top 30 cities, not the full distribution across all 150 metro areas.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main claim made by the ranking?
2. What are the cases in the dataset?
3. What variables are included in the composite score?
4. Why might someone disagree with the 80% / 20% weighting choice?
5. What is the difference between saying Ann Arbor is ranked first and saying Ann Arbor's score is far above the second-place score?
6. What does the infographic make easy to see, and what does it make hard to see?
7. Should a ranking like this be used to make decisions about where to live, where to study, or where to invest? What additional data would you want?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

WalletHub's ranking gives Educational Attainment 80% of the total score and Quality of Education & Attainment Gap 20%. Explain in two or three sentences how this weighting affects the meaning of "most educated city."

### Calculation Prompt

The top five total scores are 94.48, 85.00, 82.67, 81.93, and 79.62. Calculate the score gap between ranks 1 and 2 and between ranks 2 and 3. Which adjacent rank gap is larger, and what does that show about rankings?

### Data Biography Prompt

Identify the cases, at least four variables, and at least two data sources behind this ranking. Then write one limitation of using a composite score to summarize education across metro areas.

### Exit Ticket

In one sentence, explain why a ranking can be mathematically precise but still debatable.

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies metropolitan areas as the cases.
- Distinguishes rank from score.
- Explains that the ranking depends on metric choice and weights.
- Names at least one limitation of composite indexes or map-based communication.
