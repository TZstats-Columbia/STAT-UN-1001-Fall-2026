## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story. It is readable, concrete, and introduces the core puzzle clearly: overall international enrollment changed only a little, while new-student enrollment dropped much more sharply.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A late-2025 report on U.S. colleges found that total international enrollment fell only 1%, but the number of new, first-time international students fell 17%. That difference makes this a strong introductory statistics story. Two claims can both be true at once because they use different groups and different definitions. Students can ask who is being counted, why continuing students matter, and how one summary can hide a much larger change inside an important subgroup. The story is also useful for thinking about percentages, composition, and why a stable overall total does not always mean a stable situation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* especially well because it looks simple at first and then becomes a lesson in denominator choice. "International enrollment is steady" and "new international enrollment is down sharply" sound contradictory, but they summarize different quantities. Total enrollment includes continuing students and students in Optional Practical Training, while the 17% decline refers only to new first-time students.

That makes the story useful for teaching weighted averages, subgroup analysis, and survivorship-like issues. A class can discuss how a stable total may be sustained by one subgroup even while another subgroup changes dramatically. It also opens careful discussion about whether students should interpret the decline as a short-term shock, a policy effect, a measurement issue, or some combination. The data support description first; stronger causal claims require more evidence.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: colleges, students, or student subgroups.
- Variables: student status, academic level, year, and enrollment change.
- Counts versus percentages.
- Denominator choice.
- Subgroup analysis.
- Weighted averages.
- Composition effects.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Two Claims, Both True

Ask students to explain how total international enrollment can fall only 1% while new international enrollment falls 17%. Have them describe what kind of student population could produce that pattern.

### Build A Toy Dataset

Give students a hypothetical table with continuing students, new students, and students in post-graduation work programs. Ask them to compute the overall change and then the subgroup changes.

### What Is The Denominator?

Have students rewrite several statements in clearer statistical language, including "17% drop in new students" and "1% drop overall," making the denominator explicit each time.

### College-Level Variation

Ask students why some colleges could experience much larger declines than the national summary suggests. Then discuss why national averages can hide meaningful local variation.

## Discussion Questions

1. What population is being described by the 1% decline?
2. What population is being described by the 17% decline?
3. Why are those two statistics not contradictory?
4. How can continuing students make the overall picture look more stable than the new-student picture?
5. Why might a national average hide serious problems for some campuses?
6. What additional data would you want before claiming that one policy definitely caused the decline?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why a 1% drop in total international enrollment and a 17% drop in new international enrollment can both be correct.

### Concept Check

In the statement "new, first-time international students fell 17%," who belongs in the denominator?

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason the overall total can stay fairly stable even when one important subgroup declines sharply.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: interpreting a percentage.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: What does a 17% decline in new, first-time international students mean?
- Answer: It means the number of students entering U.S. colleges from abroad for the first time was 17% lower than in the previous year.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: subgroup versus overall summary.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Why does a 1% decline in total international enrollment not tell the whole story?
- Answer: Because the overall total combines different subgroups, and one subgroup can change a lot even when the combined total changes only slightly.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: denominator choice.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Which is the better denominator for studying arrival of new international students: all current international students or only first-time international students? Why?
- Answer: Only first-time international students, because that denominator matches the question about new arrivals.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: composition effects.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Give one way the number of students staying for temporary work after graduation could affect the national total.
- Answer: It can keep total international enrollment relatively high even if fewer new students arrive.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: hidden heterogeneity.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A student says, "Since the national decline was only 1%, colleges probably are not affected much." What is a better response?
- Answer: National averages can hide large declines at individual colleges, especially if the effects differ by institution type or student level.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A headline says, "Policy X caused a 17% drop in international enrollment." What is one statistical reason to be cautious?
- Answer: The data show a change over time, but they do not by themselves isolate one cause from other factors such as visa delays, economic conditions, or changing student preferences.

## Teaching Note

This story is particularly effective in a course with many students who are themselves thinking about college pathways, graduate school, or international education. It gives a concrete example of why overall summaries are often too blunt to answer the real question.
