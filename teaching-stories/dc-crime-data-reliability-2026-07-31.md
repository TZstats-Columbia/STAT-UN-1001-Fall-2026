## Student-Facing Paragraph

In late July 2026, Washington, D.C.’s inspector general reported that the police department’s crime-statistics system had experienced a long-running breakdown in internal controls. That makes this a strong statistics story because the headline issue is not just crime, but data quality. If incidents are misclassified or reviewed inconsistently, then charts, trends, and before-versus-after comparisons can become misleading. Students can use the story to ask what exactly is being counted, how classification rules affect totals, and why weak measurement systems can distort public debates even before anyone starts arguing about causes.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* especially well because it moves students upstream from interpretation to measurement. The AP reported on July 28, 2026 that the DC inspector general reviewed crime data from 2015 through 2025 and found a prolonged breakdown in internal controls, while not finding evidence of fraud or deliberate manipulation. The report highlighted classification errors, incomplete explanations passing review, and the loss of an earlier independent review function.

That makes the story useful for showing that data quality problems can arise without anyone fabricating numbers. Students often assume a table or graph is a clean representation of reality. This case lets an instructor slow down and ask how incidents become rows in a dataset, how offense categories are assigned, and how review procedures affect reliability.

The story also supports cautious interpretation of trend claims. If a city reports that one category of crime fell or another rose, students should ask whether definitions, coding practices, staffing, or review rules changed. The case is accessible because students do not need legal background. They only need the basic statistical idea that if measurement changes, comparisons may stop meaning what people think they mean.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Count Of What?

Give students a small mock table of incident reports with ambiguous descriptions. Ask them to classify each event into offense categories, then compare how different groups coded the same cases.

### Trend With A Broken Ruler

Show a hypothetical five-year crime trend and then reveal that classification rules changed in year three. Ask students which comparisons still seem trustworthy and which need caution.

### Audit The Dashboard

Have students list questions they would ask before trusting a public crime dashboard: Who enters the data? Who reviews it? What gets corrected later? Are categories stable across years?

### Measurement Versus Causation

Ask students to separate two claims: “recorded burglaries fell” and “the city became safer.” Then discuss why the second claim needs more than the first.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why is a crime statistic not the same thing as crime itself?
2. How could two officers looking at the same incident produce different coded outcomes?
3. Why does inconsistent classification make year-to-year comparisons less trustworthy?
4. What is the difference between bad data quality and intentional fraud?
5. If a city changes how it reviews police reports, what should readers ask before comparing new data with older data?
6. What other kinds of public data might have similar measurement problems?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why a decline in recorded crimes does not automatically prove that the true number of crimes declined by the same amount.

### Concept Check

A dataset becomes more useful for trend analysis when:

- A. categories are reviewed consistently each year
- B. categories are renamed frequently
- C. officers use different definitions across shifts
- D. unclear reports are left uncorrected

Correct answer: A.

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason that a public crime dashboard can be misleading even if no one intentionally falsified data.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: measurement versus reality.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: A city reports 12% fewer robberies this year than last year. What is one reason the true change in robberies could be different from 12%?
- Answer: Reports may have been classified differently across the two years, so the recorded count may not perfectly reflect the true change.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: reliability.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: definition.
- Prompt: What does reliability mean in a setting where officers code incident reports into crime categories?
- Answer: It means similar incidents are coded consistently across officers and across time.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: administrative data limitations.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why is “no evidence of fraud” not enough to conclude the dataset is fully trustworthy?
- Answer: Because a dataset can still contain classification errors, inconsistent review, and incomplete documentation even without intentional manipulation.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: comparability over time.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Suppose a department restores an independent review unit in 2026. Why might 2025 to 2026 comparisons require caution?
- Answer: Because the measurement process changed, so part of any difference may come from better coding rather than a real change in crime.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: operational definitions.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why should a student ask for the definition of a “crime category” before interpreting a graph?
- Answer: Because the count depends on how incidents are defined and classified, and different rules can change the totals.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: study design thinking.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: application.
- Prompt: Give one way an outside auditor could evaluate whether a police dataset is being coded consistently.
- Answer: The auditor could review a sample of incident reports, have independent coders classify them, and compare their classifications with the department’s recorded categories.
