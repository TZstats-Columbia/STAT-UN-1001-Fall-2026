## Student-Facing Paragraph

A recent AP News story reports that about 1 billion more people now experience at least one day each year of dangerous heat stress than did in the 1970s. The estimate comes from combining weather records and population data, using a definition of heat stress that tries to capture what heat feels like to the human body rather than temperature alone. This is a useful statistics story because it turns a big global claim into measurable quantities while raising important questions about definitions, data sources, uncertainty, and how averages can hide large differences across places.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

The story focuses on a large-scale estimate of change over time: how many more people are exposed to dangerous heat stress now than several decades ago. That makes it a strong fit for introductory statistical reasoning because students can see how a headline number depends on choices about measurement, thresholds, time periods, and the unit of analysis. The AP summary also makes clear that the analysis blends climate data with population data, which helps students think about where numbers come from and how derived variables are constructed.

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* especially well because students can identify cases and variables, distinguish a count from a rate, interpret a model-based estimate, and discuss why a global total should not be mistaken for a uniform experience. It also supports discussion of uncertainty and cautious causal reasoning: long-term warming is part of the explanation, but local exposure also depends on population growth, geography, housing, and infrastructure.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the claim, the cases, and the measured or derived variables.
- Week 2: Discuss how operational definitions affect what counts as "dangerous heat stress."
- Week 3: Compare totals, rates, and subgroup differences across places or periods.
- Week 4: Practice cautious causal language with observational and model-based evidence.
- Week 5: Use the story to discuss probability, risk language, and the difference between exposure and certainty of harm.
- Week 6: Treat counts of dangerous-heat days or exposed people as random quantities influenced by climate variability and measurement choices.
- Week 7: Discuss uncertainty around global estimates and why a large total can still have a confidence range.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define The Variable

Ask students whether the variable of interest is temperature, humidity, heat stress, dangerous-heat days, or number of people exposed. Use this to show how different definitions can change the story.

### Count Versus Rate

Have students compare the statement "1 billion more people" with alternatives such as "a larger share of the world's population." Discuss what each version reveals and what each hides.

### Threshold Sensitivity

Ask what might happen to the estimate if the threshold for "dangerous" heat stress were moved slightly up or down. This helps students see that some headline numbers depend strongly on cutoffs.

### Sketch A Better Graph

Students design a simple graph showing change from the 1970s to today, then discuss what extra information would be needed to show uncertainty or regional variation.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?
2. What are the cases, and what are the key variables?
3. Why is "dangerous heat stress" a more complicated variable than air temperature alone?
4. What is the difference between saying more people are exposed and saying a higher proportion of people are exposed?
5. Why might a global estimate hide important differences across countries or cities?
6. What kinds of uncertainty remain even when the story reports a very large number?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

### Exit Ticket

Why is "1 billion more people" not enough by itself to describe the full statistical story?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: derived variable.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: Is "dangerous heat stress" in this story best treated as a directly observed raw value or a derived measure based on conditions such as temperature and humidity?
- Answer: A derived measure.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: count versus rate.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If more people are exposed to dangerous heat stress now than in the 1970s, then the share of the population exposed must also have increased by the same amount.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage increase.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: Suppose a region had 200 million people exposed in the 1970s and 300 million today. What is the percent increase?
- Answer: The increase is 100 million. The percent increase is 100/200 = 50%.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpretation of global totals.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Explain in one or two sentences why "1 billion more people exposed" does not mean every place experienced the same change.
- Answer: The global total combines very different places, so some regions may have seen much larger increases and others much smaller changes. A total does not show the distribution across locations.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: thresholds and sensitivity.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why might the estimate change if researchers use a slightly different threshold for what counts as "dangerous" heat stress?
- Answer: Because people near the cutoff may move into or out of the exposed category, changing the total count.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better graph design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a display.
- Prompt: Describe one graph that would help students see both the long-term increase and the uneven regional distribution in this story.
- Answer: A strong answer proposes a time series plus a regional comparison, or another graph that shows both change over time and variation across regions.
