## Accessible Student Source

Use the Office for Students press release together with the NSS results page. The story is accessible without much background knowledge and gives students concrete numbers to interpret: a 71.8% response rate, 361,953 respondents, rising positive responses overall, and persistent differences across student subgroups.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

Results released on July 8, 2026 from the UK National Student Survey said that 71.8% of eligible final-year undergraduates responded, and that more than 361,000 students took part. Overall responses improved on several measures, but disabled students remained less positive than non-disabled students across all survey themes, and older students were more positive than younger students. This makes a good statistics story because it asks students to think about who answered the survey, what "more positive" means, how subgroup comparisons can reveal patterns hidden by overall averages, and why a strong response rate still does not erase questions about representation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it combines a large survey with careful interpretation problems that students can actually see. The headline is broadly upbeat: nearly three quarters of eligible final-year undergraduates responded, and positive responses increased across all themes compared with 2025. But the same release also says older students were more positive than younger students, and disabled students were less positive than non-disabled students across all themes.

That makes the story useful for teaching several foundational ideas at once. Students can distinguish the full target population from the responding sample, discuss what a 71.8% response rate does and does not guarantee, and examine how subgroup differences matter even when the overall average improves. Because the survey questions vary slightly across UK nations, the story also opens a nice discussion of measurement and comparability.

The story is also useful for slowing down broad claims such as "students are happier now." A careful reader should ask: which students, on which questions, in which countries, and compared with what baseline? That is exactly the kind of disciplined interpretation an introductory course should develop.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Who Answered?

Ask students to identify the target population, the responding sample, and at least two groups who may still be underrepresented despite a high response rate.

### Rewrite The Headline

Have students rewrite the upbeat headline in a statistically precise way that keeps the subgroup gaps visible.

### Overall Improvement, Unequal Experience

Give students the overall positive trend together with the statement about disabled students reporting less positive experiences. Ask them to explain why both statements can be true.

### Compare Measures

Discuss what it means for "teaching on my course" and "the student voice" to have different positive-response percentages. Ask whether those should be treated as measuring the same thing.

## Discussion Questions

1. What does a 71.8% response rate tell you, and what does it not tell you?
2. Why might a survey with more than 361,000 respondents still have representation problems?
3. Why is it important that disabled students reported less positive experiences across all survey themes?
4. How can overall improvement hide meaningful subgroup differences?
5. Why should students be careful when comparing results across countries if some questions differ?
6. What additional information would help you judge whether the survey results represent all final-year students fairly?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why an overall increase in positive survey responses does not necessarily mean every student subgroup had the same improvement.

### Concept Check

In this story, what is the sample: all final-year undergraduates in the UK, or the students who responded to the National Student Survey?

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason a high response rate still does not eliminate the possibility of nonresponse bias.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: population versus sample.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: identification.
- Prompt: Who is the sample in the 2026 National Student Survey?
- Answer: The sample is the students who responded to the survey, not all eligible final-year undergraduates.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: response rate.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: What does a 71.8% response rate mean?
- Answer: It means 71.8% of eligible final-year undergraduates completed the survey.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: nonresponse bias.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Why can a survey with a high response rate still be biased?
- Answer: If the students who did not respond differ systematically from respondents, the results may still misrepresent the full population.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: subgroup analysis.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A student says, "Since the overall results improved, every group must have had the same positive experience." What is a better response?
- Answer: Overall improvement can happen even when some groups report less positive experiences than others.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: operational definitions.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why does the phrase "responded positively" require interpretation?
- Answer: Because it summarizes answers to specific survey questions and scoring rules rather than describing a single direct measurement.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: comparability.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why should a student be cautious about comparing every NSS result across all four UK nations?
- Answer: Because some questions differ by nation, so not every percentage is measuring exactly the same thing everywhere.
