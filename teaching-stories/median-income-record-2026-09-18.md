# A record median income: did everyone get a raise?

## Student-facing paragraph

A September 2026 Census Bureau release reports that inflation-adjusted median U.S. household income reached $87,460 in 2025, up 2.6% from 2024. Does that mean every household received a raise? A median describes the middle of a distribution, not what happened to each household. Consider what additional information you would want before saying that everyone—or any particular family—was better off. [Source: Census Bureau release](https://cdn.www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026/income-poverty-health-insurance-coverage.html)

## Synopsis and instructor context

The release gives a comparable 2024 median of $85,210. Its figures come from the 2026 Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplement, asking about the prior calendar year. The weighted response rate was 61.3%; survey weights include nonresponse adjustments. The release identifies both sampling and nonsampling error. [Source: Census Bureau release](https://cdn.www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026/income-poverty-health-insurance-coverage.html)

The classroom hook is the gap between a national summary and individual experiences. Students can explore that gap with invented data before discussing the survey. Keep the distinction between household income and a person's wages explicit. Do not interpret two annual medians as the median of individual household changes, or attribute a change to a particular policy from this comparison alone. No student should need to disclose personal or family income; all activities use fictional households.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students identify cases and variables, interpret a measure of center, compare distributions, evaluate the scope of a claim, and explain why a survey estimate can be uncertain. Suitable for an early unit on summaries and statistical claims, with a later return during sampling and inference.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- **Cases and variables:** For the income distribution, cases are households and income is quantitative; survey respondents provide information about households and their members.
- **Median versus mean:** A middle value does not describe the entire distribution or its extremes.
- **Aggregate versus individual change:** Changes in summaries do not identify changes for particular cases.
- **Measurement and comparability:** Comparing dollars across years requires attention to purchasing power; comparing households also raises questions about household size and composition.
- **Sampling and representation:** Nonresponse can matter if respondents and nonrespondents differ in relevant ways. Weighting can reduce bias but is not a guarantee that all bias disappears.
- **Uncertainty:** A large sample does not eliminate systematic measurement or selection problems.
- **Causal scope:** A before–after national comparison alone does not isolate a cause.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Two distributions, 10 minutes.** Give five fictional households, A–E, the following annual incomes in thousands of comparable dollars. These are invented teaching data, not Census observations.

   | Household | Year 1 | Year 2 |
   |---|---:|---:|
   | A | 20 | 18 |
   | B | 40 | 38 |
   | C | 60 | 66 |
   | D | 80 | 76 |
   | E | 100 | 110 |

   Find each year's median and mean. Count the households whose incomes rose. Draw two dot plots on the same scale. **Instructor key:** medians 60 and 66; means 60 and 61.6; only two of five households gained. The median rose 10% while three households lost income. The median household change is −2 thousand, unlike the +6 thousand change in medians.

2. **Audit the percentage, 5 minutes.** Using the published medians, calculate (87,460 − 85,210) / 85,210 × 100. **Key:** approximately 2.64%, consistent with a rounded 2.6%. Explain why this is a percentage change in dollar amounts, not a percentage-point change.

3. **Recruitment thought experiment, 8 minutes.** Imagine a survey invitation sent only through a workplace email system. Which households might be missed? Then ask whether a larger number of responses fixes that problem. This is a hypothetical design, not how the CPS recruits respondents. Groups propose an improved sampling frame and one way to investigate remaining nonresponse.

4. **Headline workshop, 5 minutes.** Rewrite “Everyone got richer last year.” Require a named summary, a household rather than worker interpretation, and a limit on the conclusion. Compare rewrites without asking students to take a policy position.

## Discussion questions

- What can a median tell us, and what would a histogram add?
- How could household income rise without any household member receiving a wage increase?
- Why should dollar amounts from different years be put on a comparable basis?
- What distinguishes a sample that is large from a sample that represents its target population?
- What evidence would help assess whether nonresponse affects estimated income?
- What extra design or evidence would be needed to credit a particular policy for a change?

## Example assessment instruments

**Multiple choice, 1 point:** A country's median household income rises. Which conclusion must follow? (A) Every household gained income. (B) The mean rose by the same percentage. (C) The estimated middle of the household income distribution rose. (D) Most individuals received raises. **Key:** C.

**Calculation and interpretation, 4 points:** Using the fictional table, calculate the change in median income and the percentage of households with an increase. Explain why the results are compatible. **Key:** +6 thousand (1); +10% relative change in the median (1); 40% of households increased (1); the median's movement does not require most individual households to gain (1).

**Short response, 3 points:** A student says, “Enough responses will remove all nonresponse bias.” Evaluate this statement. **Rubric:** rejects the claim (1); explains that missing households may differ systematically in income (1); distinguishes more precise sampling from correction of systematic bias (1).

**Exit ticket, 2 points:** Name one additional statistic or graph that would help interpret a rising median and explain its purpose. **Example key:** income percentiles or a histogram (1), with an explanation that these reveal where gains or losses occur in the distribution (1). Other well-justified answers accepted.
