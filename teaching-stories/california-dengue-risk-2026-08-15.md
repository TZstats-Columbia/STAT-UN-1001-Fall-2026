## Student-Facing Paragraph

A June 2026 university news story reported on a study suggesting that warming temperatures could make more parts of California suitable for local dengue transmission. The researchers estimated that about 18.2 million Californians, or 46% of the state population, already live in places with the conditions needed for local transmission, and that the number could grow in the future. This is a useful statistics story because the numbers come from a model rather than a direct count of future cases, so students can ask what assumptions went into the estimate, what "risk" means here, and how uncertainty enters when researchers combine climate, mosquito, travel, and population data.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it helps students distinguish observed data from model-based estimates. The researchers were not counting future dengue cases directly. Instead, they combined three ingredients needed for local transmission: likely mosquito presence, temperatures suitable for transmission, and introductions from travel-associated cases. That setup is intuitive for beginners and opens a useful conversation about how a quantity can be estimated indirectly.

The story is also relatable. Students understand mosquitoes, weather, travel, and maps of where people live. The accessible article explains that dengue is still rare in California, that many infections are asymptomatic, and that most California cases still come from travel rather than local spread. That balance makes it ideal for discussing why a large at-risk population estimate is not the same as a prediction that all those people will get sick.

This story also illuminates assumptions and possible bias. The researchers note that their model may underestimate risk if fast-growing areas are not fully reflected, and one assumption links travel-related introduction risk to demographic patterns connected to places where dengue is endemic. That gives students a concrete chance to discuss proxies, measurement limits, and why model outputs should be interpreted cautiously.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Count Versus Model

Ask students to make a two-column list of quantities in the story that are directly observed and quantities that are modeled or projected.

### What Does "At Risk" Mean?

Students write one sentence explaining why living in an area with conditions for transmission is not the same as being guaranteed to get dengue.

### Assumption Audit

Have students identify one assumption in the model and explain how changing it might increase or decrease the estimated population at risk.

### Public-Health Headline Check

Students rewrite a dramatic headline into a statistically careful one that keeps the main result but clarifies that the estimate is conditional on model assumptions.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is being estimated in this study, and what is not being directly counted?
2. Why is 46% of Californians at risk not the same claim as 46% will get dengue?
3. What role do travel-associated cases play in the researchers' reasoning?
4. Why might a model use proxy measures instead of perfect direct measurements?
5. What kinds of uncertainty remain even if the mathematics of the model are correct?
6. What new data over the next few years would help test the model's usefulness?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain the difference between a direct count of dengue cases and a model-based estimate of people living in areas suitable for transmission.

### Concept Check

Which statement best describes the quantity "18.2 million Californians live in an area with conditions for local dengue transmission"?

- A. It is a direct tally of Californians who already had dengue
- B. It is a model-based estimate of people living in suitable-risk areas
- C. It is the number of mosquitoes counted by researchers
- D. It is proof that local outbreaks will occur everywhere in those areas

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Name one assumption that could affect a model estimating dengue risk in California.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: percentages and counts.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: The story says 18.2 million people is 46% of Californians. What do those two numbers describe?
- Answer: They describe the estimated number and share of Californians living in areas with conditions suitable for local dengue transmission.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: direct versus indirect measurement.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: Is the 18.2 million figure a direct count or a modeled estimate?
- Answer: It is a modeled estimate.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: risk interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why is it misleading to say "46% of Californians will get dengue" based on this study?
- Answer: The study estimates where conditions for transmission exist, not how many people will actually become infected.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: proxy variables.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Why might researchers use travel-associated case patterns when estimating local dengue risk?
- Answer: Because local transmission depends partly on infected travelers introducing the virus, and those introductions are difficult to observe directly in advance.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: omitted variables and assumptions.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Give one factor that could make the model underestimate or overestimate future risk.
- Answer: Population growth or changing travel patterns could change the number of people exposed and alter the estimated future risk.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious inference.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: A policymaker says, "The model proves California will have widespread dengue outbreaks soon." What is a statistically better response?
- Answer: The model suggests increasing suitability for local transmission, but actual outbreaks still depend on mosquito presence, introductions, human behavior, surveillance, and other uncertain factors.

## Teaching Note

This story is especially useful when students are first learning that many important public-health numbers are estimated indirectly and should be read as conditional, not absolute.
