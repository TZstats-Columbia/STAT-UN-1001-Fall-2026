## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story from August 26, 2026. It is readable for non-specialists and gives students several concrete numbers to interpret, including the number of visits studied, the temperature cutoff for "extreme heat," and examples of diagnoses that became more common.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A 2026 study linked very hot days in Chicago to more emergency-room and urgent-care visits for many problems beyond classic heat stroke, including kidney issues, skin conditions, some injuries, and some mental-health-related visits. Researchers analyzed about 916,900 visits from 2011 to 2023 and compared patterns on days at or above 92.6 degrees Fahrenheit. This is a useful statistics story because it helps students think about how researchers define exposure, link data sources together, interpret associations, and avoid jumping from a pattern in observational data to a simple causal claim.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it uses a large observational dataset in a city context that students can understand immediately. The central claim is not that heat causes every medical problem listed, but that certain diagnoses were more common on extreme-heat days than on other days. That distinction creates a clean opening for discussion of association, comparison, and cautious language.

The story also works well for measurement. Researchers had to define what counted as an extreme-heat day, decide what the cases were, and connect medical records with temperature data by time and place. Students can see that none of those choices are automatic. Even before formal inference, they can ask whether a threshold like 92.6 degrees is reasonable, whether Chicago is representative of other places, and whether diagnosis codes capture the full health burden of heat.

This story is also useful because it shows how large datasets can still have limitations. A sample of nearly a million visits sounds definitive, but it is still observational, local, and dependent on coding practices in health records. That makes it a good example of why "big data" does not eliminate the need for careful statistical reasoning.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the main claim and the evidence used to support it.
- Week 2: Define cases, variables, and units such as visits, patients, diagnoses, and days.
- Week 3: Interpret counts, rates, and thresholds in plain language.
- Week 4: Distinguish observational studies from experiments.
- Week 5: Discuss how the comparison between hotter and less hot days is constructed.
- Week 6: Consider operational definitions such as "extreme heat."
- Week 7: Explain why association does not by itself prove causation.
- Week 8: Brainstorm confounders such as season, neighborhood conditions, and access to cooling.
- Week 9: Discuss measurement limits in diagnosis codes and health records.
- Week 10: Consider external validity and whether Chicago generalizes to other cities.
- Week 11: Ask what subgroup patterns might matter by age, neighborhood, or housing conditions.
- Week 12: Connect descriptive evidence to public-health and city-planning decisions.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-3 for data, variables, studies, and interpretation; later material on comparison, confounding, and cautious inference.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define The Study

Ask students to identify the cases, explanatory variable, and response variable in everyday language.

### Threshold Debate

Have students discuss what changes if researchers define extreme heat using a different cutoff than 92.6 degrees Fahrenheit.

### Causal Language Repair

Students revise an overstrong headline like "Heat causes crime and kidney disease" into wording that better matches the evidence.

### What Is Missing?

Students list additional variables that could help explain why some neighborhoods or groups may be more affected than others.

## Discussion Questions

1. What exactly was compared in this study?
2. Why is this an observational study rather than an experiment?
3. What does the temperature threshold add, and what might it hide?
4. Why does a large number of visits not automatically prove a causal claim?
5. What kinds of confounding factors could help explain some of the patterns?
6. What would you want to know before generalizing these findings to another city?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why the study supports an association between extreme heat and some diagnoses, but not a blanket causal claim for every diagnosis listed.

### Concept Check

Which statement is best supported by the evidence?

- A. Every emergency-room visit on a hot day was caused by heat
- B. Some diagnoses were more common on extreme-heat days in this Chicago dataset
- C. The study proves the same pattern must hold in every U.S. city
- D. Diagnosis codes completely measure the health effects of heat

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Name one possible confounding factor or measurement issue that could affect how the results are interpreted.

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies the study as observational.
- Describes the comparison in plain language.
- Avoids overstating causation.
- Mentions at least one limitation or missing variable.
