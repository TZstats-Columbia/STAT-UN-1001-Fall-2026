## Student-Facing Paragraph

Many schools are banning cell phones because they hope students will pay more attention, feel better, and do better academically. A large recent study found a more complicated picture: phone use in class went down sharply, but test scores and attendance did not quickly improve. This is a good statistics story because it asks us to separate a policy's intended effect from what the data actually show. It also asks what outcome we care about most: phone use, grades, attendance, discipline, well-being, or classroom climate.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story focuses on school phone bans and whether they achieve the outcomes people expect. Many parents, teachers, and policymakers hope that reducing phone use will improve attention, grades, attendance, behavior, and well-being. Recent reporting on a large study describes a mixed pattern: phone use fell sharply, while test scores and attendance did not show clear immediate improvement.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it shows why a vague claim like "phone bans work" needs to be translated into measurable variables. Students can identify the population, explanatory variable, outcome variables, comparison groups, and possible confounders. It also creates a natural opening for cautious conclusions: the same policy may show strong evidence for one outcome, weak evidence for another, and different patterns over time.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Outcome variable: phone use, test score, attendance, suspension, self-reported well-being.
- Explanatory variable: school phone-ban policy.
- Comparison group: schools with and without strict bans, or before and after a ban.
- Confounding: schools adopting strict bans may differ from other schools.
- Practical significance: a large drop in phone use may not imply a large change in grades.
- Time trend: effects may differ in year 1 versus year 3.
- Measurement: phone pings, surveys, school records, and test scores measure different things.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Claim Autopsy

Give students the claim "Phone bans improve schools." Ask them to rewrite it into three more precise claims, each with a measurable outcome.

### Outcome Sorting

Students sort possible outcomes into categories: behavior, academic achievement, mental health, classroom climate, and implementation. Then they decide which outcomes could be measured with school records, surveys, or device data.

### Before/After Caution

Show a hypothetical graph of test scores before and after a phone ban. Ask students what else could have changed at the same time and why a comparison group matters.

### Policy Memo Exit Ticket

Students write three sentences to a school board: what the study suggests, what it does not prove, and what data the board should keep collecting.

## Discussion Questions

1. What does it mean for a policy to "work"?
2. Which outcome should matter most when evaluating a school phone ban?
3. Why might phone use decrease while test scores stay about the same?
4. What variables would you collect if Columbia wanted to evaluate a phone policy in discussion sections?
5. Why is a before/after comparison not automatically causal evidence?
6. How should policymakers respond when a study shows mixed results?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

A school reports that after a phone ban, the percentage of students seen using phones in class fell from 60% to 15%, but average test scores stayed about the same. Write a careful conclusion in two or three sentences. Your answer should mention at least two outcome variables.

### Study Design Prompt

Design a simple study to evaluate a phone ban. Identify the population, sample, explanatory variable, at least two outcome variables, and one possible confounder.

### Mini-Rubric

- States a precise claim rather than a vague claim.
- Identifies outcome variables clearly.
- Distinguishes reduced phone use from improved learning.
- Notes at least one limitation or possible confounder.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: explanatory versus response variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: In this story, is the school phone-ban policy best treated as an explanatory variable or a response variable?
- Answer: Explanatory variable.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: causal overclaim.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If phone use drops after a ban but test scores do not improve, then the policy definitely failed.
- Answer: False. The conclusion depends on which outcomes matter and over what time frame.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage-point change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: A school reports phone use in class fell from 60% of observed students to 15% after a ban. What is the percentage-point change?
- Answer: 15% - 60% = -45 percentage points, so phone use fell by 45 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting mixed results.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Write a two-sentence interpretation of this result: phone use fell sharply, but attendance and test scores changed little.
- Answer: A strong answer says the policy appears effective for reducing visible phone use. It also says the evidence does not show clear short-term improvement in every academic outcome.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Name one confounder that could complicate a comparison between schools with strict phone bans and schools without them.
- Answer: A strong answer names a factor such as school culture, prior discipline patterns, resources, or student demographics.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: policy evaluation design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Design a better study of whether phone bans improve academic outcomes. State the comparison groups, at least two outcomes, and one reason your design would be stronger than a simple before/after comparison.
- Answer: A strong answer uses comparable schools or repeated data with a clear comparison group, includes outcomes such as scores and attendance, and explains how the design reduces confounding.
