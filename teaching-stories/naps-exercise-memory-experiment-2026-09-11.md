## Student-facing paragraph

Could a nap or exercise help memory after lost sleep? A McGill report describes 54 healthy young adults who stayed awake for 30 hours, then received a cycling session, a nap opportunity, or a control condition. They viewed images after the intervention and took a memory test three days later. The report describes roughly 22% better performance with exercise or napping than with the control. Before turning that result into advice, ask: What did “memory” mean, who participated, and what exactly was compared? [Student source](https://healthenews.mcgill.ca/workout-or-nap-either-can-help-your-sleepless-brain-study-finds/)

## Synopsis and instructor context

The paper verifies random assignment to 20-minute exercise, 90-minute nap, or 20-minute control conditions. Behavioral analyses included 53 participants: 18, 18, and 17 respectively. The primary memory measure was the proportion of previously seen images correctly recognized. Participants were ages 18–35; night-shift workers were excluded. The exercise–nap difference was not statistically significant. [Original research](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2528246123)

This is a useful introduction to separating causal comparisons within an experiment from generalization beyond it. Unequal intervention durations also invite discussion of what treatment package is being compared. Avoid treating a nonsignificant difference as proof of equivalence. Keep neuroscience optional; all proposed activities concern design and interpretation. Use paper exercises, not student sleep deprivation.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students practice identifying cases and variables, explaining random assignment versus random sampling, interpreting a numerical claim, and writing a conclusion whose scope matches the evidence. Suitable after introducing experiments and before formal inference.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- Cases: individual participants; multiple image responses are measurements within a participant.
- Variables: assigned condition (categorical), recognition proportion (quantitative), and participant characteristics.
- Operational definitions: a specific recognition task does not measure every form of memory.
- Chance variability: random assignment does not guarantee identical groups.
- Relative percentage change versus percentage-point difference.
- Generalization and uncertainty: sample eligibility and imprecision matter.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Design map, 8 minutes:** Draw recruitment → random assignment → intervention → measurement. Circle where selection could limit representation and where randomization supports comparison.
2. **Percentage translation, 5 minutes:** Use invented scores of 50% and 61%. Calculate the percentage-point difference and relative increase. Label these as illustrative values, not reconstructed study results.
3. **Headline revision, 7 minutes:** Rewrite “Exercise replaces sleep for everyone.” Require a named population, outcome, and comparison, plus one limitation.
4. **Optional chance simulation:** Shuffle 12 invented participant scores into two groups repeatedly. Compare mean differences to see why a difference can arise even when assignment is random. This is a separate toy experiment, not a reanalysis.

## Discussion questions

- Does random assignment imply that participants represent all students or workers?
- Would an image-recognition result necessarily predict exam grades or safe driving?
- What would a comparison with a normally rested group add?
- If two treatments have similar estimated outcomes, what uncertainty remains?
- Why might counting each image response as an independent participant exaggerate the amount of information?

## Example assessment instruments

**Multiple choice, 1 point:** Random assignment primarily helps (A) make the sample representative, (B) reduce systematic preexisting differences between conditions, (C) eliminate all chance variation, or (D) prove effects in every population. **Key:** B.

**Calculation, 2 points:** In the invented example, recognition rises from 50% to 61%. Give both changes. **Key:** 11 percentage points (1); 22% relative increase, since 11/50 = 0.22 (1).

**Short response, 4 points:** Explain why an experiment may support a causal comparison yet provide limited evidence for night-shift workers. **Rubric:** random assignment supports comparability (1); distinguishes assignment from recruitment (1); identifies population/setting mismatch (1); proposes direct evidence from the target population (1).

**Exit ticket, 2 points:** Does no statistically significant exercise–nap difference establish equal effectiveness? **Key:** No (1); uncertainty can include meaningful differences, and equivalence requires additional evidence (1).
