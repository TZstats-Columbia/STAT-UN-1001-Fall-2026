## Accessible Student Source

Use the AP News article. It is easy to read, concrete, and especially good for helping students notice the difference between what was measured directly and what was inferred.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

News coverage of a recent study says rat problems are getting worse in many cities and that warmer temperatures may be part of the reason. But the researchers did not count every rat. Instead, they analyzed long-running complaint and sighting data from 16 cities and looked for trends over time. That makes this a strong statistics story because the headline feels simple, but the underlying evidence depends on proxy measurement, comparisons across places, and careful interpretation of what complaint data can and cannot show.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story works well because it starts with a relatable urban problem and then quickly opens up several core statistical questions. The study uses complaint reports and sighting records as a proxy for rat infestation, which gives students a natural way to talk about measurement validity. A rise in complaints could reflect more rats, but it could also reflect changing human behavior, easier reporting systems, or different city practices.

The story also helps students distinguish trend claims within cities from level comparisons across cities. Washington, D.C. may show one of the fastest increases in reports over time, but that does not necessarily mean it has the most rats overall. The article is therefore a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it combines cases and variables, proxy measurement, confounding, comparisons over time, and caution about causal interpretation.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the statistical claim, cases, and variables.
- Week 2: Discuss how complaint reports differ from direct counts.
- Week 3: Compare within-city trends versus between-city levels.
- Week 4: Practice cautious causal reasoning with observational data.
- Week 5: Use the story to discuss chance variation and risk language in public-health or nuisance reports.
- Week 6: Treat city-year complaint totals as random quantities that can vary with reporting behavior as well as rat activity.
- Week 7: Discuss uncertainty in trend estimates when the measured outcome is only a proxy.
- Week 8: Frame hypothesis-testing questions about whether complaint increases exceed expected variability or reporting noise.
- Week 9: Distinguish statistically detectable trends from practical importance for city response.
- Week 10: Compare proportions or categories of cities with rising versus stable complaint patterns.
- Week 11: Use numerical comparisons of average complaint changes across cities or regions.
- Week 12: Revisit the story through association, prediction, and the limits of ecological and administrative data.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-2 for variables and data quality; Ch. 5-8 for uncertainty, comparison, and association.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: city-year observations or complaint records, depending on dataset structure.
- Variables: city, year, number of complaints or sightings, temperature trend, population density, and urbanization measures.
- Proxy measurement.
- Construct validity.
- Time trends.
- Comparison versus association.
- Confounding.
- Administrative data limits.
- Ecological reasoning and cross-city comparison.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Proxy Or Direct Measure

Ask students whether "number of rat complaints" is the same thing as "number of rats." Then have them list reasons the two might move together and reasons they might not.

### Design A Better Study

Have students propose what additional data they would want if they were a city trying to estimate rat infestation more directly, such as trap counts, inspection records, camera data, or neighborhood sanitation measures.

### Trend Versus Level

Write two statements on the board: "City A has the fastest increase in complaints" and "City A has the most rats." Ask which statement the study supports and which it does not.

### Confounding Brainstorm

Students identify alternative explanations for rising complaint counts besides rising rat populations, such as app-based reporting, media attention, sanitation policy changes, or population growth.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in the story?
2. What are the cases and what are the main variables?
3. Why are complaint reports a proxy rather than a direct measure?
4. How could reporting behavior create bias in the observed trend?
5. Why is a fast increase within one city not the same as having the highest rat population overall?
6. What additional evidence would make the climate explanation more convincing?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The story reports that rat complaints increased in many cities. Explain one reason that more complaints do not automatically prove there are more rats.

### Cases And Variables Prompt

Suppose the underlying dataset has one row per city per year. Identify the cases and name at least four variables that might appear in the data.

### Exit Ticket

What is one possible confounder that could affect the relationship between warming temperatures and rat complaints?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: proxy measurement.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: In this story, are rat complaints a direct measure of the rat population or a proxy measure?
- Answer: A proxy measure.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: trend versus level.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If a city has one of the fastest increases in rat complaints, it must have the largest rat population overall.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percent increase.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: A city recorded 400 rat complaints in one year and 520 the next year. What is the percent increase?
- Answer: The increase is 120 complaints. The percent increase is 120/400 = 30%.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting proxies.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Explain in one or two sentences why more complaints do not automatically prove there are more rats.
- Answer: Complaint counts can also change because people report more often, reporting systems improve, or attention increases. The proxy may move for reasons other than the underlying rat population.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Name one factor other than rat population growth that could increase complaint counts over time.
- Answer: A strong answer names a factor such as easier app-based reporting, media attention, population growth, or changes in sanitation policy.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better measurement design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Propose one stronger data-collection strategy for estimating rat infestation more directly than complaint records alone.
- Answer: A strong answer suggests adding direct indicators such as trap counts, inspections, camera monitoring, or standardized field surveys.

## Teaching Note

This story pairs especially well with an early class on "what are the data?" because it forces students to separate a vivid headline from the actual recorded variable. It can also be reused later when discussing administrative data, measurement error, or ecological comparisons.
