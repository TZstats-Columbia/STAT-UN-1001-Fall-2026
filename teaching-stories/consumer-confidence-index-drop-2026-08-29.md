## Student-Facing Paragraph

In late August 2026, the Conference Board reported that its Consumer Confidence Index fell from 90.2 in July to 89.4 in August, while a separate expectations measure fell more sharply to 68.2. The numbers came from a survey conducted August 3 to August 16 and were summarized in news coverage as a sign that Americans were feeling worse about the near future even while views of current conditions improved. This is a useful statistics story because students can ask what an index measures, how a survey turns many responses into one headline number, and how much meaning should be attached to a small month-to-month change.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it turns a familiar headline about "consumer confidence" into a set of basic statistical questions. Students can ask who was surveyed, what questions were asked, how answers were combined into an index, and whether a change from 90.2 to 89.4 is substantively important or just a small fluctuation.

The story is especially useful for interpreting summaries and composite measures. Many students are comfortable with percentages but less comfortable with an index scaled to a base year. This gives an opportunity to explain that some public statistics are not direct percentages or counts. They are constructed summaries, which means students must learn to ask how the summary was built before drawing conclusions from it.

This is also a good setting for discussing uncertainty, wording, and representation. The survey was conducted during a specific time window in August, and public sentiment can shift quickly. Students can consider whether responses depend on current events, whether the sample represents all adults equally well, and whether the headline "confidence falls" sounds more dramatic than the actual numerical change.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Decode The Index

Ask students to explain why an index value like 89.4 is not the same thing as saying 89.4 percent of people feel confident.

### Small Change Or Real Change?

Students debate whether a drop from 90.2 to 89.4 deserves a strong headline without seeing more information about uncertainty or recent history.

### Rebuild The Headline

Have students rewrite "consumer confidence plunges" into language that matches the size of the observed change.

### Compare Measures

Students compare the overall index with the expectations index and discuss why different summaries can move in different directions.

## Discussion Questions

1. What does the story mean by consumer confidence?
2. Why is an index different from a simple percentage or count?
3. Is the change from 90.2 to 89.4 large enough to matter on its own?
4. How could the survey dates affect the results?
5. Why might current conditions improve while future expectations worsen?
6. What other data would you want before claiming that the economy is clearly getting better or worse?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why students should ask how an index is constructed before interpreting a month-to-month change.

### Concept Check

Which statement is best supported by the evidence?

- A. Exactly 89.4 percent of Americans are confident in the economy
- B. The survey proves gas prices caused the change in confidence
- C. The August confidence index was slightly lower than the July index
- D. Every subgroup in the country became more pessimistic by the same amount

Correct answer: C.

### Exit Ticket

Give one reason a small change in an index may not justify a dramatic headline.

### Mini-Rubric
