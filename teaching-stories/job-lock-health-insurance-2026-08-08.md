## Accessible Student Source

Use the Gallup story from July 21, 2026. It is short, clear, and organized around a concrete headline number that students can interrogate: what exactly counts as "job lock," who was asked, and whether the measure captures all forms of employment constraint.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A July 2026 Gallup report said that about 1 in 4 U.S. workers have stayed in a job they wanted to leave because they were worried about losing employer-sponsored health insurance. This is a strong statistics story because the claim sounds simple, but it depends on survey wording, definitions, and population boundaries. Students can ask who counts as a worker, whether gig workers or part-time workers were included, how strongly "wanted to leave" was measured, and whether this percentage should be interpreted as a broad national estimate or as a rough indicator of a hard-to-measure experience.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a good fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it connects a headline percentage to survey design and interpretation. Gallup reported that 24% of workers said they had stayed in a job they wanted to leave because of concerns about losing health insurance. The same release reported subgroup differences, including higher rates among younger adults and lower-income workers, which gives students accessible comparison questions.

The variable being measured is also interesting. "Job lock" is not directly observed the way age or salary is. It is a self-reported experience built from a survey item about employment choices, preferences, and perceived risk. That makes the story useful for discussing operational definitions, response bias, and how social experiences become data.

The story also supports cautious causal reasoning. The survey shows that workers say health-insurance concerns influenced job decisions. It does not, by itself, establish the full economic effect of insurance policy on labor mobility, because many related factors such as wages, family responsibilities, chronic illness, and local job availability may also matter.

- Week 3: Translate "1 in 4" into percentages and counts.

- Week 5: Compare reported rates across age or income groups.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define The Variable

Ask students whether "job lock" is a single directly observed variable or a constructed self-report measure. Then have them propose one alternative wording that might capture a similar idea differently.

### Build A Two-Way Table

Provide a simple hypothetical table crossing age group with job-lock response and ask students to compute conditional percentages and compare groups.

### Who Counts As A Worker?

Have students list categories that may or may not be included in the survey population, such as freelancers, part-time workers, unpaid family workers, and recently unemployed people. Then discuss how those choices change interpretation.

### Headline Audit

Students revise the headline "One in four workers experience job lock" into a version that keeps the result but adds one methodological caveat.

## Discussion Questions

1. What exactly is Gallup trying to measure with the phrase "job lock"?
2. Why might a self-reported measure of wanting to leave a job be hard to capture perfectly?
3. How could the percentage differ if the survey population included unemployed adults who recently left jobs?
4. Why is a subgroup difference interesting but not automatically causal?
5. What other factors besides health insurance might shape whether someone stays in a job?
6. What additional data would help you study actual job mobility rather than reported feelings or intentions?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: percentage interpretation.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: translation.
- Prompt: What does "1 in 4 workers" mean as a percentage?
- Answer: It means about 25% of workers.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: cases and variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: definition.
- Prompt: In this Gallup survey, what are the cases?
- Answer: The cases are the individual workers who responded to the survey.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: operational definition.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why is "stayed in a job they wanted to leave" not as straightforward a variable as age?
- Answer: It depends on personal judgment, memory, and interpretation, so different respondents may apply the idea differently.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: subgroup comparison.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Suppose younger workers report job lock more often than older workers. Give one possible explanation that does not assume age itself is the only cause.
- Answer: Younger workers may differ in income, job benefits, family situation, or type of employment, any of which could affect the response.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: population boundaries.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why does it matter whether gig workers and part-time workers were included in the survey population?
- Answer: Because the reported percentage only generalizes to the group that was actually surveyed, and those categories may have very different insurance situations.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: A student says, "This proves employer-sponsored insurance causes workers to stay in bad jobs." What is a better statistical response?
- Answer: The survey suggests many workers perceive insurance concerns as an important factor, but it does not isolate causation from wages, family needs, health status, local labor markets, or other influences.

## Teaching Note

This story is especially effective when the course is introducing the idea that socially important variables are often measured indirectly and therefore require close attention to wording and population definition.
