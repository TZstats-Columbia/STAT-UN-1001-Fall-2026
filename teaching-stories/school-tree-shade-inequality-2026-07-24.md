Use the Guardian article as the main student-facing reading. It is accessible and concrete, and it gives students several clear comparisons to interrogate without requiring technical background in GIS or education policy.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A July 2026 analysis reported that private schools in England have 41% more tree cover near school buildings than state schools, and that state schools with the highest levels of free school meal eligibility have less tree cover than more affluent state schools. This is a strong statistics story because it turns a familiar idea, that some schools are greener and cooler than others, into measurable comparisons. It also invites students to ask how tree cover was measured, what free school meal eligibility is being used as a proxy for, and whether averages can hide large differences across places.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because it combines education, environment, and inequality in a form that students can understand quickly. The core statistical claims are simple to state: private schools had more tree cover nearby than state schools, and among state schools, those serving more economically disadvantaged students tended to have less tree cover.

But the story gets more interesting once students slow down. Tree cover was measured within a 100-meter radius of school locations, not by asking whether a school "feels green." Free school meal eligibility is used as an indicator of socioeconomic disadvantage, which is useful but imperfect. Students can therefore discuss operational definitions, proxy variables, and how different measurement choices might change the conclusion.

This story also helps students think about comparison groups and confounding. Rural schools tend to have more tree cover than urban schools, so a simple overall comparison may blend school type, geography, and deprivation. That makes it a strong example for showing why an observed association should not be treated as a complete explanation.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### What Counts As "Greener"?

Ask students how they would measure whether one school is greener than another. Then compare their ideas with the article's 100-meter-radius tree-cover measure.

### Proxy Check

Discuss why free school meal eligibility is being used in the story. Ask students what it captures well and what it may miss about school or neighborhood disadvantage.

### Hidden Variables

Give students the claim that private schools have 41% more tree cover than state schools. Ask them to list other variables that could help explain part of that difference, such as rural location, campus size, or land ownership.

### Better Graph

Have students sketch a graph that would show both the private/state difference and the variation among state schools by deprivation level.

## Discussion Questions

1. What are the cases and key variables in this story?
2. Why is free school meal eligibility a proxy rather than a direct measure of wealth or deprivation?
3. Why might a school's location matter when comparing tree cover?
4. What does the claim "41% more tree shade" tell you, and what does it leave out?
5. Why could median tree cover be more informative than only reporting one overall average?
6. What additional data would help you judge whether the inequality is mainly about school type, neighborhood wealth, or geography?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain one reason the comparison between private and state schools might be affected by confounding variables.

### Concept Check

In this story, what does free school meal eligibility stand in for?

### Exit Ticket

Name one additional variable you would want before making a stronger claim about why some schools have more tree cover than others.

### Mini-Rubric

- Explains at least one proxy variable.

- Interprets comparison language cautiously.

## Teaching Note

This story is useful because students immediately recognize schools as real places, but the statistical questions are richer than they first appear. It is especially good for teaching proxy variables and the risks of reading too much causation into a descriptive comparison.
