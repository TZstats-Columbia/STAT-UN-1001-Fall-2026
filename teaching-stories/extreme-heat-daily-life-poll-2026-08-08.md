## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story from August 3, 2026. It is easy to read, grounded in everyday examples like outdoor plans and electric bills, and it gives students concrete percentages they can interpret and question without needing technical climate science.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

In early August 2026, the Associated Press reported results from an AP-NORC poll showing that extreme heat is affecting everyday life for many Americans. People reported changing routines, worrying about energy costs, and dealing with limits on outdoor activity. This is a useful statistics story because the claims are based on a survey rather than direct physical measurements, so students can ask who was surveyed, how terms like "extreme heat" or "affected" were defined, whether experiences differ across places, and how strongly a poll can support broad claims about the country.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it centers on survey data, population claims, and careful interpretation of percentages. The AP article reported that about 6 in 10 Americans say extreme heat has affected their daily life at least a little, including 3 in 10 who say it has affected them a lot or some. Students can immediately work with counts, proportions, wording, and subgroup comparisons.

The topic is also relatable. Even students who know little about climate policy understand hot days, changed plans, and higher utility bills. That makes it easier to focus class attention on representation, question wording, recall, and whether a self-reported experience variable measures the same thing for all respondents.

The story is especially useful for distinguishing a measurable descriptive claim from a causal one. A poll can describe how many respondents report being affected, but it is less suited to establishing exactly why one person feels more affected than another. That opens discussion of confounding by region, housing quality, income, work conditions, age, and access to air conditioning.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Turn Percentages Into People

Give students a hypothetical sample of 1,200 adults and ask them to convert reported percentages into approximate counts, then discuss why exact counts in a poll story are usually estimates rather than census totals.

### What Does "Affected" Mean?

Ask students to list at least three different ways a respondent might interpret being "affected by extreme heat." Then discuss how a vague but relatable survey item can still be useful while remaining imperfect.

### Subgroup Comparison Design

Have students propose two subgroup comparisons they would want to see in the poll, such as South versus Northeast or outdoor workers versus indoor workers, and explain why those comparisons might matter.

### Poll Critique Rewrite

Students rewrite one AP sentence so it communicates both the finding and one limitation, such as sampling uncertainty or self-report ambiguity.

## Discussion Questions

1. What population is the AP-NORC poll trying to describe?
2. Why might two people living in the same city answer the heat question differently?
3. What is gained and lost by using a survey instead of direct temperature or utility-bill data?
4. Why is "6 in 10 say they were affected" not the same as "6 in 10 experienced the same level of risk"?
5. What kinds of people might be underrepresented or harder to reach in a national poll about heat?
6. What additional data would help you move from description toward stronger explanation?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why a national poll can support a claim about how common heat-related disruption feels, but not fully explain why disruption differs from one person to another.

### Concept Check

If 60% of respondents say extreme heat affected their daily life, that number is:

- A. a census count of all Americans
- B. a sample statistic used to estimate a broader population quantity
- C. proof that exactly 60% of all Americans had the same experience
- D. a direct physical measurement like temperature

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason a self-reported survey response about heat might differ from a direct environmental measurement.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: sample versus population.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: definition.
- Prompt: In a national poll about extreme heat, what are the cases?
- Answer: The cases are the individual survey respondents.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: interpretation of proportions.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: computation.
- Prompt: If 60% of a sample of 1,200 adults say extreme heat affected daily life, about how many respondents is that?
- Answer: About 720 respondents.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: wording and measurement.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: Why might the phrase "affected daily life" introduce measurement variability?
- Answer: Different respondents may interpret "affected" differently, such as canceled plans, discomfort, health risk, or higher bills.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: representation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: reasoning.
- Prompt: Give one reason the experiences in a national poll could differ by region even if the question wording is the same.
- Answer: Regions differ in climate, housing, infrastructure, air-conditioning access, and outdoor work patterns.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: A student says, "People with higher electric bills must be the ones reporting more heat disruption." Why is that too strong?
- Answer: Other factors such as income, housing quality, region, household size, and health conditions could affect both bills and reported disruption.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious inference.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Why does a poll showing many Americans feel affected by heat not by itself prove that one particular local heat policy would solve the problem?
- Answer: The poll describes experiences and concerns, but it does not isolate which interventions work or rule out other causes of the reported impacts.

## Teaching Note

This story works especially well early in the semester because students can analyze percentages, wording, and representativeness without needing much background knowledge beyond everyday experience with hot weather.
