## Student-facing paragraph

A University of Florida summary describes surveys of more than 22,000 pet adopters from 112 shelters and rescues in 40 states. Over 94% rated adoption positively at each survey time, yet more than 78% of dog adopters and 50% of cat adopters reported a challenge. These findings need not contradict each other: someone can be happy with an adoption while needing help. What questions would you ask before interpreting “satisfaction” as evidence that everything went smoothly? [Student source](https://sheltermedicine.vetmed.ufl.edu/2026/02/20/shelter-to-home-surveys-of-early-post-adoption-experiences-with-more-than-22000-dog-and-cat-adopters/)

## Synopsis and instructor context

Surveys were sent on Day 1, Week 1, and Month 1. Positive ratings meant 4 or 5 out of 5. Responses were included only when the pet remained in the home. These details make measurement and inclusion rules central to interpretation. [University summary](https://sheltermedicine.vetmed.ufl.edu/2026/02/20/shelter-to-home-surveys-of-early-post-adoption-experiences-with-more-than-22000-dog-and-cat-adopters/)

The teaching opportunity is to distinguish two variables that can coexist and to investigate who is missing. Do not assume the same respondents answered each wave or combine headline percentages into an observed joint table. The summary does not establish a national probability sample. This complements the archive's animal stories by focusing on satisfaction measurement and survey inclusion, rather than bites, population counts, or detection.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students translate everyday claims into measurable variables, reason about overlapping categories, examine denominators, and distinguish a large sample from a representative sample. The story requires no pet ownership experience; similar reasoning applies to housing, courses, or customer-service surveys.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- Unit of analysis: distinguish an adopter, an adoption, and a response at a particular time.
- Ordinal ratings and a threshold that turns them into a binary summary.
- Joint versus marginal distributions: satisfaction and challenges are separate variables.
- Coverage, nonresponse, and exclusion rules.
- Repeated measurements and changing denominators.
- Descriptive comparisons do not identify causes of differences between species.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Two-way table, 10 minutes:** Give this invented single-wave sample of 100 adopters: 75 satisfied with a challenge, 20 satisfied without a challenge, 5 dissatisfied with a challenge, and 0 dissatisfied without a challenge. Have students compute the margins and explain why 95% satisfied and 80% challenged can both be true. These are teaching values, not study data.
2. **Survey redesign, 8 minutes:** Write separate questions about overall experience and specific support needs. Specify response options, recall period, and who receives follow-up.
3. **Missing voices, 7 minutes:** Draw a funnel from all adoptions to eligible survey responses. At each stage, identify a possible loss and explain why the direction of bias cannot automatically be known.
4. **Graph critique:** Draw separate bars for satisfaction and challenges from the invented table. Explain why a pie chart treating the two as mutually exclusive would mislead.

## Discussion questions

- Can someone report both satisfaction and difficulty? What other settings illustrate this?
- How might excluding pets no longer in the home change interpretation?
- Why does geographic breadth alone not establish representativeness?
- What denominator and time point would you need before comparing two percentages?
- Could changes between survey waves reflect different respondents rather than changes within people?

## Example assessment instruments

**Multiple choice, 1 point:** High satisfaction and frequent challenges imply (A) the data must be wrong, (B) the variables can overlap, (C) challenges cause satisfaction, or (D) everyone responded. **Key:** B.

**Table exercise, 3 points:** Using the invented table, find the proportion satisfied, the proportion with challenges, and the proportion with challenges among satisfied adopters. **Key:** 95/100 = 95%; 80/100 = 80%; 75/95 ≈ 78.9%. One point each, requiring the denominator.

**Short response, 4 points:** Evaluate “A very large sample proves that nearly all adopters nationwide are satisfied.” **Rubric:** rejects sample size as sufficient for representativeness (1); identifies coverage or nonresponse (1); identifies the pet-still-in-home inclusion rule (1); offers a conclusion limited to respondents and survey timing (1).

**Exit ticket, 2 points:** What extra information is needed to claim that individual satisfaction increased over time? **Key:** linked responses from the same adopters (1), with attention to missing follow-ups or attrition (1).
