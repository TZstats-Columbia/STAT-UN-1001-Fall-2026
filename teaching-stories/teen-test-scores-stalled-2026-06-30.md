# Teen Test Scores Stalled - 2026-06-30

## Original Source Links

- AP News: [Teens' reading and math scores have stagnated, US test results show](https://apnews.com/article/test-scores-middle-school-reading-math-78dd0dadbebccc7ba35ed04bf4be66c4), June 2026.
- The Nation's Report Card: [NAEP Long-Term Trend Assessment Results: Reading and Mathematics](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/ltt/2025/), 2025 release.

## Accessible Student Source

Use the AP News article. It is short, concrete, and easy for students to connect to questions about trends, samples, benchmarks, and interpretation.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A new national testing report says 13-year-olds in the United States did not show significant improvement in reading or math between 2023 and 2025, even though 9-year-olds improved. The story also says the 2025 reading score for 13-year-olds was not significantly different from the score in 1971. This is a strong statistics story because it asks students to interpret trend data carefully, distinguish "no significant change" from "no problem," and think about what national samples can and cannot say about all schools or all students.

## Synopsis And Instructor Context

This story is useful because the headline sounds simple, but the underlying statistical reasoning is not. The NAEP long-term trend assessment uses nationally representative samples rather than a census of all students, so every claim depends on measurement, sampling, and statistical comparison. The Nation's Report Card summary says 13-year-olds showed no significant change in reading or mathematics since 2023, while 9-year-olds improved in both subjects between 2022 and 2025. The AP story adds details that roughly 31,000 students participated across the 2024-2025 school year and that only 58% of 13-year-olds met the benchmark skill level in reading and 70% in math.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* especially well because it lets students separate several ideas that are often blurred together in news coverage: average score versus benchmark percentage, sample result versus population claim, and lack of statistically significant change versus proof of no real change. It also creates a natural opening for discussion of time trends, subgroup comparisons, operational definitions of "proficiency" or "benchmark," and caution when moving from national data to explanations about causes.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify claims, cases, variables, and populations.
- Week 2: Discuss samples, representativeness, and what a national assessment measures.
- Week 3: Interpret time trends, averages, percentages, and benchmarks.
- Week 4: Distinguish description from explanation and avoid causal overreach.
- Week 5: Use benchmark percentages to practice conditional statements and compare subgroup rates.
- Week 6: Discuss margin of error, sampling variability, and why repeated national samples will not produce identical results.
- Week 7: Compare score distributions or subgroup averages using plots and summaries rather than headlines alone.
- Week 8: Examine how changes in reading-for-fun or absenteeism could confound simple explanations of score change.
- Week 9: Contrast statistically significant change with practically important change in student outcomes.
- Week 10: Evaluate whether differences between age groups support prediction, explanation, or neither.
- Week 11: Critique causal claims about why scores changed and identify what extra data or design would strengthen them.
- Week 12: Revisit the story as a synthesis case on measurement, sampling, comparison, and cautious interpretation.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-2 for samples, variables, and summaries; Ch. 5-8 for uncertainty, significance, comparison, and association.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: individual students in the sampled assessment.
- Variables: reading score, math score, age group, year, benchmark status, reading-for-fun frequency.
- Population versus sample.
- National representativeness.
- Time trend interpretation.
- Average score versus percentage meeting a benchmark.
- Statistical significance versus practical significance.
- Operational definitions and test design.
- Cautious causal reasoning.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Headline Translation

Ask students to rewrite "scores stagnated" into one sentence that is statistically more precise and one sentence that overstates the evidence.

### Cases And Variables

Have students define the cases and list possible variables in a dataset behind this story. Then ask how the dataset changes if the case is a student versus an age-year group.

### Average Versus Benchmark

Put two statements on the board: "the average score did not change significantly" and "58% met the reading benchmark." Ask students why those statements are related but not interchangeable.

### Trend Interpretation

Students sketch a simple graph comparing 9-year-olds and 13-year-olds over time and explain what the graph suggests without making unsupported causal claims about phones, schools, or the pandemic.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?
2. What are the cases and key variables?
3. Why does it matter that the assessment uses a nationally representative sample instead of testing every student?
4. What is the difference between "no significant change" and "students are doing fine"?
5. Why is "same as 1971" a powerful statement, and what does it still not tell us?
6. What additional breakdowns or graphs would help you understand the story better?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The report says 13-year-olds showed no significant change in reading or math since 2023. Explain what that does and does not mean in plain language.

### Cases And Variables Prompt

Identify the cases, one response variable, and two other variables that could appear in the dataset behind this story. Then explain whether the article is describing a sample or a census.

### Exit Ticket

Why is it important to distinguish between an average score and the percentage of students who meet a benchmark?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: statistical significance in context.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: multiple choice.
- Prompt: The article says 13-year-olds showed no significant change in reading scores between 2023 and 2025. Which conclusion is best supported?
- Answer: B. The sample provided no strong evidence of a real average change over that period.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: cases and variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: Which pair correctly identifies a case and a variable in this story? A. Case: 2025; Variable: national sample B. Case: student; Variable: reading score C. Case: benchmark; Variable: age 13 D. Case: math; Variable: student
- Answer: B. Case: student; Variable: reading score.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: benchmark interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: multiple choice.
- Prompt: Suppose 58% of sampled 13-year-olds met a reading benchmark. Which statement is most accurate? A. Therefore 42% of all U.S. adults failed reading. B. Therefore the sample suggests a substantial share of 13-year-olds did not meet that benchmark. C. Therefore average reading scores must have risen sharply. D. Therefore every school had exactly 58% meeting the benchmark.
- Answer: B. The sample suggests a substantial share of 13-year-olds did not meet that benchmark.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: representativeness and limits of generalization.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: In one or two sentences, explain why a nationally representative sample can support a national claim but still does not tell us exactly what happened in every school.
- Answer: A representative sample can estimate national patterns, but it does not measure every school directly. Local schools may differ from the national average.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: percentage-point change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: If 70% of sampled 13-year-olds met the math benchmark and 58% met the reading benchmark, what is the percentage-point gap between the two benchmarks?
- Answer: 12 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: stronger explanation design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Propose one additional variable or comparison that would help investigate why 13-year-old scores changed differently from 9-year-old scores.
- Answer: A strong answer names a relevant variable such as absenteeism, reading time, course-taking, or cohort differences and explains how it could clarify the comparison.

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies students as the cases.
- Recognizes that the data come from a sample, not every student.
- Distinguishes trend description from causal explanation.
- Uses "significant change" carefully and accurately.
