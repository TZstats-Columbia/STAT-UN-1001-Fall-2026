## Accessible Student Source

Use the Associated Press story republished by Local 10 on August 13, 2026. It is short, concrete, and easy for students to read while still giving enough percentages and subgroup comparisons to support careful statistical questioning.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A national AP-Ipsos poll in August 2026 reported that about half of K-12 parents say at least one of their children plays an organized sport, but participation is much higher in higher-income households. About 7 in 10 parents with household income of at least $100,000 said their children play organized sports, compared with about 4 in 10 parents with lower household income. This is a useful statistics story because it turns a common social experience into questions about samples, subgroup comparisons, cost as a possible explanatory variable, and how far we can go from an association in survey data to stronger causal claims.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because the core evidence comes from a probability-based public opinion poll with clear subgroup comparisons. Students can identify the population, distinguish the overall adult sample from the oversample of sports parents, and ask why weighting was needed before reporting national estimates.

The topic is highly accessible. Most students understand youth sports, family budgets, transportation, equipment costs, and the tradeoff between wanting an activity to be available and actually being able to afford it. The article gives multiple quantities that can be interpreted in plain language, including the gap between higher-income and lower-income families, spending categories, and reported reasons children stop playing sports.

The story also supports careful reasoning about confounding and mechanism. Income may matter through fees, transportation, time flexibility, neighborhood access, school offerings, safety, or prior sports culture. That makes the story a good example of why a strong association does not by itself prove a single causal explanation.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the main descriptive claim and the population it refers to.
- Week 2: Define cases, variables, and subgroups.
- Week 3: Translate "7 in 10" and "4 in 10" into proportions and percentage-point gaps.
- Week 4: Discuss how probability sampling differs from a convenience sample.
- Week 5: Interpret subgroup comparisons carefully.
- Week 6: Explain oversampling and weighting in plain language.
- Week 7: Distinguish association from causation.
- Week 8: Consider plausible confounders behind income differences.
- Week 9: Ask what survey responses measure well and what they may miss.
- Week 10: Discuss uncertainty and margins of error for subgroup results.
- Week 11: Consider what extra data would help explain why the gap exists.
- Week 12: Connect descriptive evidence to school, parks, and recreation policy.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-3 for data, variables, and interpretation; later material on sampling, comparison, and cautious inference.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Reconstruct The Comparison

Have students restate the headline claim in fully statistical language, including who was surveyed and what groups were compared.

### Sample Design Check

Ask students why a poll of all adults and an oversample of sports parents might both be useful in the same study.

### Cost As A Mechanism

Students list at least three ways household income could be related to sports participation without income itself being the only direct cause.

### Rewrite The Headline Carefully

Students revise a headline like "Rich families care more about sports" into wording that matches what the poll actually measured.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?
2. Why is a comparison between higher-income and lower-income households an association rather than a proof of causation?
3. What does it mean that the poll used a probability-based panel and weighting?
4. Why might the results for parents of sports-playing children have a different margin of error than the results for all adults?
5. What important variables about access to sports are not captured by income alone?
6. What additional data would help explain whether money, time, transportation, or school availability matters most?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why the difference between higher-income and lower-income families in this poll should be described as an association.

### Concept Check

Which statement is best supported by the poll results?

- A. Higher income causes children to become better athletes
- B. Higher-income parents are more likely to report that their children play organized sports
- C. Organized sports eliminate inequality between families
- D. The poll proves all lower-income children want to quit sports

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Name one confounding factor that could help explain why sports participation differs by household income.

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies the comparison groups.
- Interprets the result as descriptive survey evidence.
- Mentions at least one sampling or weighting issue.
- Avoids causal overstatement.
