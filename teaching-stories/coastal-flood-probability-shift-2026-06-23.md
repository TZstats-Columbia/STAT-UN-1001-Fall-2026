## Student-Facing Paragraph

A recent AP News story explains that climate change has made some coastal floods far more likely than they used to be. The article focuses on events that historically had about a 1% chance of happening in a given year and reports that, in many places, those events are now much more common. This is a strong statistics story because it lets us unpack what a "1% annual chance" really means, how probability changes over time, and why people often misinterpret rare-event language.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

The story is built around a simple but powerful statistical idea: a probability attached to an event is not fixed forever. A "1% annual chance" flood is often described casually as a "100-year flood," which can mislead people into thinking such events are evenly spaced or cannot happen twice in a short period. The AP piece gives students a concrete example of changing risk, showing how historical baselines may no longer describe present conditions.

This fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because it supports careful work on probability language, long-run frequency, and the difference between an annual chance and a waiting time. It also opens discussion about model-based inference, how scientists compare present conditions with historical baselines, and why communication matters when public decisions depend on uncertain but shifting risks.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Translate "1% Annual Chance"

Ask students to explain in plain language what a 1% annual chance means. Then compare student explanations to the misleading phrase "100-year flood."

### Repeated-Trial Reasoning

Have students reason about whether a flood with a 1% annual chance can happen in back-to-back years. This reinforces that small probability does not mean impossible or evenly spaced.

### Relative Versus Absolute Change

If an event becomes much more likely, ask what extra information students need to judge the size of the change. This helps distinguish "twice as likely" from an actual change in annual probability.

### Risk Communication Rewrite

Students rewrite a headline or public warning so it communicates the probability more clearly to a general audience.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: annual probability.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: In this story, what does a "1% annual chance" describe?
- Answer: The probability that the flood event occurs in a given year.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: probability misconception.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If a town had a "100-year flood" this year, then another such flood cannot happen next year.
- Answer: False. A 1% annual chance in one year does not prevent the event from happening in the next year.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: expected frequency from repeated trials.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: If a flood truly has a 1% chance each year and conditions stay fixed, about how many such floods would you expect over 100 independent years on average?
- Answer: About 1 flood on average, because 100 x 0.01 = 1.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting changing risk.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Explain in one or two sentences why the phrase "100-year flood" can mislead people when climate conditions change over time.
- Answer: The phrase sounds like a fixed waiting time, but it really refers to a probability under a particular baseline. If the baseline changes, the old label may no longer describe current risk.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: relative versus absolute risk.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is the statement "flood risk doubled" incomplete without more information?
- Answer: A doubling could mean a change from 1% to 2% or from 20% to 40%, which have very different practical implications.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: risk communication design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a communication.
- Prompt: Write a two-sentence public warning that explains rising coastal flood risk more clearly than the phrase "100-year flood."
- Answer: A strong answer translates the label into annual probability, notes that current risk may be higher than the historical baseline, and avoids deterministic language.
