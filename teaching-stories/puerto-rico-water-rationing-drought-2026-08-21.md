## Student-Facing Paragraph

In August 2026, Puerto Rico began rotating water rationing in and around San Juan as drought and extreme heat pushed reservoir levels dangerously low. News reports said more than 180,000 customers would lose water for 48 hours at a time in alternating zones, while federal drought data showed widespread rainfall deficits and forecasts of continued dry, hot conditions. This is a useful statistics story because students can ask how drought is measured, how direct service interruptions differ from broader climate indicators, and how percentages, counts, and forecasts are used together when officials make practical decisions under uncertainty.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is a strong fit for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it links several kinds of evidence that students need to learn to distinguish: direct counts of affected customers, climate summaries based on observed rainfall and temperature, and forward-looking drought outlooks based on models and forecasts. That mix makes it easy to discuss what is observed, what is estimated, and what remains uncertain.

The context is concrete and relatable. Students understand running water, weather, reservoirs, and the daily consequences of being told that service may disappear for two days at a time. The story also includes simple but meaningful numeric ideas such as rainfall deficits, the number of municipalities affected, and the difference between islandwide drought conditions and the smaller set of places placed under immediate rationing.

This story also helps students reason carefully about measurement and representation. "Puerto Rico is in drought" is not one single variable with one single value. Different sources describe severity across different parts of the island, and drought conditions do not map perfectly onto how many households actually lose water service. That makes the story useful for discussing definitions, geographic variation, and why operational measures matter.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Sort The Numbers

Ask students to separate the story's quantities into three groups: directly observed counts, descriptive climate summaries, and future-looking forecasts.

### Drought Versus Rationing

Students explain why "a place is in drought" and "a place is under water rationing" are related but not identical statistical statements.

### Map The Cases

Have students identify whether the natural cases in the story are customers, municipalities, reservoirs, days, or regions, and discuss how the choice changes interpretation.

### Headline Repair

Students rewrite a simplified headline like "Puerto Rico runs out of water" into language that is statistically accurate and less misleading.

## Discussion Questions

1. What quantities in this story are direct counts, and what quantities are forecasts or broader indicators?
2. Why is a rainfall deficit not the same as a count of households without water?
3. Why might only some municipalities face immediate rationing even if drought conditions are broader?
4. What role do operational definitions of drought play in how the story is reported?
5. What infrastructure or policy factors could help explain who loses service first?
6. What additional data would you want before claiming the drought affects all residents equally?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain the difference between a direct count of customers affected by rationing and a drought outlook predicting persistent dry conditions.

### Concept Check

Which statement best matches the evidence in the story?

- A. Every place in Puerto Rico faced the same level of water shortage at the same time
- B. Drought indicators and service interruptions describe related but different things
- C. A rainfall deficit proves exactly how many families will lose water next month
- D. Forecast models directly count future water outages

Correct answer: B.

### Exit Ticket

Give one reason drought conditions across an island may not translate into identical household water outcomes everywhere.
