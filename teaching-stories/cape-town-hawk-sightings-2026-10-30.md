# When a thousand sightings do not mean a thousand birds

Prepared: 2026-10-30. New to this collection; source published 2026-10-29.

## Original source links and accessible student-facing source

- **Student reading and original university reporting:** Ridovhona Mbulaheni, [UCT project investigates thriving African harrier-hawks](https://www.news.uct.ac.za/article/-2026-10-29-uct-project-investigates-thriving-african-harrier-hawks), University of Cape Town, October 29, 2026.
- **Original project source:** [Cape Urban African Harrier-Hawk Project](https://urbanharrierhawk.org/), FitzPatrick Institute, UCT; accessed October 30, 2026. This live page may change.

## Student-facing paragraph

Residents of Cape Town are helping researchers find African harrier-hawks and their nests. UCT reports that a public appeal brought more than 1,000 sightings and helped confirm 13 nests. Those numbers count different things: several people could report the same bird, and some nests may remain unseen. The researchers describe 13 as a minimum, rather than the total number of nests in the city. How could the team use residents’ knowledge while checking whether some neighborhoods are easier to observe than others?

## Synopsis / instructor context

This is an ongoing research project, not a completed population census or a published causal study. Its public reporting system welcomes uncertain identifications and requests photographs or video for checking. The project website also notes that repeat observations can help locate territories. Use these features to discuss the value of repeated records alongside the danger of treating them as independent animals. No specialist bird knowledge is needed. The distinctive teaching focus is designing a citizen-reporting system and separating its units; existing wildlife stories in the collection address other projects.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students will identify observational units, define measurable variables, distinguish a collected sample from a target population, and qualify conclusions using the data-collection process. Suitable for a 20–30 minute introductory lesson before formal inference.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- **Cases and variables:** A report, a bird, a nest, and a neighborhood are different possible cases. A report-level table might contain date, approximate location, photo availability, and verification status.
- **Representation:** Ask whether opportunity to observe, access to reporting, and awareness of the project could differ by neighborhood. These are possible biases to investigate, not demonstrated findings about this project.
- **Repeated observations:** Multiple records can provide useful corroboration without increasing the count of distinct birds or nests.
- **Measurement error:** An uncertain report can be checked; accepting leads and confirming nests are separate decisions.
- **Uncertainty:** A verified minimum does not supply an upper bound or a confidence interval for the total population.
- **Comparison:** More reports after publicity might reflect increased reporting, increased animal abundance, or both.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Choose the case (5 minutes).** Groups sketch separate report-level and nest-level tables. Require a unique ID and three variables in each. Discuss how to connect reports to nests without counting the same nest repeatedly.
2. **Compare two neighborhoods (10 minutes).** Use these entirely fictional classroom data, unrelated to actual Cape Town observations:

   | Neighborhood | Reports | Volunteer observation hours | Distinct confirmed nests |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|
   | A | 60 | 30 | 4 |
   | B | 30 | 10 | 3 |

   Draw one bar chart of report counts and another of reports per hour. A has twice as many reports, but rates are 2 versus 3 reports per hour. Neither chart alone estimates nest density: search conditions, repeat reports, area, and detection may differ. Even the verified nest counts need comparable coverage before a population comparison.
3. **Improve the design (10 minutes).** Propose a supplement to public reports: randomly select map cells, observe at comparable times for fixed durations, record searches with no sightings, and verify suspected nests. Explain how this improves coverage while acknowledging that hidden nests can still be missed. Conduct this as a paper exercise using invented locations.

## Discussion questions

- What would you need to know before interpreting 1,000 reports as 1,000 different birds?
- Why can a neighborhood with no reports still contain nests?
- What does a confirmed minimum tell us? What does it leave unknown?
- How could publicity change a graph of sightings over time?
- Which is the better variable for studying breeding: reports submitted, confirmed active nests, or chicks leaving nests? Explain how the answer depends on the question.

## Example assessment instruments

**Exit ticket (4 points).** A headline says, “Public finds the city’s entire hawk population: 13 nests.” Identify two problems and write a defensible replacement.

*Key:* Nests are not individual birds (1); confirmed nests need not include all nests (1). A replacement should state that the project confirmed at least 13 nests (1) and avoid claiming a complete population census (1).

**Calculation and interpretation (4 points).** Using the fictional table, calculate reports per observation hour for each neighborhood. Does the larger rate establish more nests per square kilometer?

*Key:* A = 2 (1); B = 3 (1). No (1): area, detection, repeated reports, or search conditions remain unaccounted for (1).

**Study-design response (4 points).** Suggest two changes to make neighborhood comparisons more informative and explain each.

*Key:* Award one point per concrete change and one per explanation, up to four. Examples: sample locations across the city to improve coverage; standardize search time to make effort comparable; record unsuccessful searches to retain the denominator; validate reports to reduce misidentification. Do not award full credit for “collect more data” without specifying how.
