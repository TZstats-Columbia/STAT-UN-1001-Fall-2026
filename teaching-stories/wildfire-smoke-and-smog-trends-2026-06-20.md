## Student-Facing Paragraph

For years, air pollution rules helped reduce smog in the United States. But a recent report says that since 2015, smoke from larger wildfires has started pushing smog back up again. The article reports that national smog levels fell from 2003 to 2015, then rose after 2015, and that many people were exposed to unhealthy ozone levels during recent heavy smoke seasons. This is a good statistics story because it shows how a broad trend can reverse, how measurement depends on where monitors are located, and how researchers use data to make cautious claims about health impacts.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story centers on a recent study arguing that wildfire smoke has reversed some of the gains the United States made in reducing ground-level ozone, or smog. The AP article reports several usable introductory statistics facts: ozone fell by 11% from 2003 to 2015, then increased by 4% after 2015; EPA monitors cover only a small share of land area; and researchers combined monitor, satellite, weather, and pollution data to estimate national patterns.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it lets students distinguish observed trends from causal claims, think about how incomplete measurement affects inference, and compare percentages, rates, and population exposure. It is also a strong example of why summaries depend on the unit of analysis: counties, grid cells, years, smoke days, or exposed people can all be reasonable "cases" depending on the question.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: years, locations, monitor sites, grid cells, or people exposed to smoke.
- Variables: ozone level, wildfire smoke exposure, year, region, monitor presence, deaths attributed.
- Time trend and trend reversal.
- Measurement coverage and undercoverage.
- Model-based estimation from incomplete data.
- Absolute versus relative change.
- Association versus causation.
- Population exposure versus local measurement.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Trend Interpretation

Give students a simple two-period summary: ozone down 11% before 2015, up 4% after 2015. Ask them to write one careful sentence that does and does not overclaim what happened.

### Measurement Map Discussion

Have students sketch what could go wrong if air monitors cover mainly urban areas while smoke affects large rural areas too. Ask what kinds of places might be underrepresented.

### Graph Critique

Show a line graph of national ozone averages over time and ask students what extra information they would want: uncertainty bands, regional breakdowns, wildfire years, or the number of monitors.

### Cases And Variables Exercise

Ask small groups to define one row of a dataset for this story in two different ways, such as "one county-year" versus "one day-location," and list variables for each version.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?
2. What counts as a case if we want to study smoke and smog?
3. Why does limited monitor coverage matter for the conclusion?
4. What is the difference between a 4% rise after 2015 and a claim that every place got worse?
5. Does this story show that climate change caused all of the smog increase? Why or why not?
6. What graph would best help a general audience understand the trend?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The article says national smog levels fell by 11% from 2003 to 2015 and then increased by 4% after 2015. Explain in two or three sentences what those numbers do and do not show about air quality change over time.

### Study Design Prompt

Suppose you want to study whether wildfire smoke is associated with higher ozone levels in the Midwest. Identify the cases, at least three variables, and one limitation of relying only on EPA monitors.

### Exit Ticket

Why might a nationwide estimate based on monitors, satellites, and weather data be more informative than using monitor data alone?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: undercoverage.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: If air monitors cover only a limited set of places, what measurement issue does that create?
- Answer: Undercoverage.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: causal caution.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If national smog rose after 2015, then every region in the country must have gotten worse.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage change interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: The story says ozone fell 11% from 2003 to 2015 and later rose 4% after 2015. Which change is larger in magnitude?
- Answer: The 11% decline is larger in magnitude than the 4% increase.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting incomplete measurement.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Why can combining monitor, satellite, and weather data be more informative than using monitor readings alone?
- Answer: Because monitors do not cover every location, while combining sources can help estimate conditions in places without monitors.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: units of analysis.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Give two reasonable choices for what counts as a case in a dataset about this story.
- Answer: A strong answer names possibilities such as year, county-year, monitor-day, grid cell, or exposed person.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better study design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Design a simple observational study to examine whether smoke-heavy days are associated with higher ozone levels in one region.
- Answer: A strong answer identifies a unit such as day-location, includes smoke exposure and ozone as key variables, and notes a limitation such as incomplete coverage or weather confounding.
