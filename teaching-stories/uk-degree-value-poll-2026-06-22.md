# UK Degree Value Poll - 2026-06-22

## Original Source Links

- The Guardian: [Third of people no longer believe degree is worth the time or money, UK poll shows](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/jun/02/shrinking-graduate-premium-sours-views-on-university-poll-shows), June 2, 2026.
- National Centre for Social Research: [British Social Attitudes](https://natcen.ac.uk/british-social-attitudes), long-running survey project referenced in the article.

## Accessible Student Source

Use the Guardian article. It is accessible, gives a clear time trend, and naturally leads students to ask questions about survey wording, trends, and subgroup interpretation.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

A recent UK news story reports that the share of people who say a university degree is not worth the time and money rose from 14% in 2005 to 34% in 2025. The same report says the share who think graduates end up much better off financially fell from 50% to 36%. This is a useful statistics story because it takes a broad opinion about education and turns it into survey data that can be graphed, compared over time, and questioned in terms of wording, sampling, and interpretation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story centers on a long-running social survey rather than an experiment or administrative database. That makes it especially good for introductory statistical reasoning. Students can inspect what exactly is being measured: not whether a degree objectively pays off, but what survey respondents believe about its value. The trend over two decades is substantial, but the meaning of that trend still depends on who was asked, how the question was phrased, whether the measure stayed comparable over time, and what outside changes in tuition, labor markets, and public debate may have influenced the responses.

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it makes the distinction between an opinion measure and an economic reality especially visible. It also provides a natural context for discussing trend graphs, population and sample, operational definitions, and the difference between descriptive survey results and stronger causal claims about why public opinion changed.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the claim and clarify what the survey is actually measuring.
- Week 2: Discuss surveys, sampling, and representativeness.
- Week 3: Interpret percentages over time and compare absolute changes.
- Week 4: Separate observed opinion changes from causal explanations.
- Week 5: Use subgroup or trend statements to practice conditional language about opinions and beliefs.
- Week 6: Treat the observed survey proportion as a sample-based random quantity that would vary across repeated polls.
- Week 7: Interpret margins of error or confidence intervals for long-running survey estimates.
- Week 8: Ask whether changes over time are large enough to stand out from sampling variability.
- Week 9: Distinguish statistical significance from practical or political importance in opinion trends.
- Week 10: Use the story for inference with proportions and categorical comparisons across years or groups.
- Week 11: Contrast opinion data with numerical outcome data such as earnings, debt, or employment.
- Week 12: Revisit the story for generalization, prediction, and communication of trend claims.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-2 for surveys and variables; Ch. 5-7 for uncertainty and categorical inference.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: survey respondents.
- Variables: opinion about degree value, opinion about financial payoff, age, education, and time.
- Survey measurement and wording.
- Population versus sample.
- Trend interpretation.
- Absolute change versus relative change.
- Representation and subgroup comparison.
- Association versus causal explanation.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### What Is The Variable?

Ask students whether the story measures the economic value of college or public beliefs about that value. Use this to reinforce the distinction between a concept and its measurement.

### Trend Graph Reading

Put the 2005 and 2025 percentages on the board and ask students to describe the change in plain language. Then ask them to compute both the percentage-point change and the relative increase.

### Survey Critique

Have students brainstorm how the answers might change if the question said "financially worth it" instead of "worth the time or money."

### Causal Explanation Check

Give students a sentence such as "AI caused people to lose faith in university." Ask them to explain why the survey alone cannot support that claim.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim in this story?
2. What are the cases and variables?
3. Why is this story about public opinion rather than a direct measure of college returns?
4. Why might question wording matter here?
5. What can a time trend show, and what can it not show by itself?
6. What additional data would help if you wanted to compare beliefs about college with actual earnings outcomes?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The article says the share of people who think a degree is not worth the time and money rose from 14% in 2005 to 34% in 2025. Explain what this change describes and name one reason to be cautious when interpreting it.

### Interpretation Prompt

Is the survey measuring the actual economic return to college, public opinion about college, or both? Explain your answer in one or two sentences.

### Exit Ticket

Why is it incorrect to use this survey alone to conclude that university has become a bad investment for everyone?

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies respondents as the cases.
- Distinguishes beliefs from objective outcomes.
- Interprets time trends accurately.
- Avoids turning an opinion survey into a causal claim.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: what is being measured.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: Is this survey primarily measuring the actual economic return to college or public opinion about college?
- Answer: Public opinion about college.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: causal caution.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: A rise in the share saying college is not worth the time and money proves that college has become a bad investment for everyone.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage-point change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: The share saying a degree is not worth the time and money rose from 14% in 2005 to 34% in 2025. What is the percentage-point increase?
- Answer: 20 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting trend data.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Write one sentence describing what the reported change from 14% to 34% shows and one sentence describing what it does not show.
- Answer: A strong answer says the survey reports a substantial rise in this opinion over time. It does not show why opinions changed or whether the opinion is objectively correct.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: wording effects.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why might answers differ if the question asked whether college is "financially worth it" instead of "worth the time and money"?
- Answer: The wording shifts the concept being measured and may lead respondents to weigh earnings differently from broader personal or social value.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: connecting surveys to other data.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Propose one way to combine survey opinion data with labor-market data to study whether beliefs about college match economic outcomes.
- Answer: A strong answer specifies both an opinion measure and an outcome measure such as earnings or employment, then explains how comparing them could reveal agreement or mismatch.
