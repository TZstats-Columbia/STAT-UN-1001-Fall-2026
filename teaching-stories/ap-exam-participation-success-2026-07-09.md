Use the CT Insider story together with the College Board report. The news story gives students an easy entry point, and the College Board page supplies the percentages, counts, and definitions needed for careful interpretation.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

AP exam scores came out on July 6, 2026, and the surrounding coverage highlighted two national numbers from the College Board: 37.0% of U.S. public high school graduates in the class of 2025 took at least one AP exam during high school, and 24.8% scored a 3 or higher on at least one AP exam. Those numbers sound straightforward, but they raise classic statistics questions. Who is counted in the denominator? Why is "took an AP exam" different from "scored a 3 or higher"? What do statewide comparisons actually measure? This makes a strong introductory statistics story because students can work with percentages, rates, operational definitions, access versus success, and cautious interpretation of rankings.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story works well in *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it starts with familiar education data and quickly opens into questions about measurement and comparison. Students can distinguish between participation and performance, see why percentages can be more informative than raw counts, and examine how a single student may take several exams but count only once in some summaries.

It also supports a useful discussion of representation and fairness. The College Board report defines success partly as the share of a graduating class scoring 3 or higher on at least one AP exam, which blends access and performance into one statistic. That is a defensible measure, but it is not the only one. Students can compare alternative summaries, think about who is missing from the data, and discuss why differences across states or schools should not be explained too quickly by a single causal story.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: graduates, AP exam takers, or states, depending on the analysis.
- Variables: whether a graduate took an AP exam, whether they scored 3 or higher, state, and number of exams taken.
- Counts versus percentages.
- Difference between percentage and percentage-point change.
- Operational definitions.
- Denominator choice.
- Selection and access.
- Representation.
- Comparability across groups.
- Cautious interpretation of rankings.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Two Different Percentages

Ask students to explain why 37.0% and 24.8% answer different questions. Then have them describe what additional information would be needed to compute the percentage of AP exam takers who scored 3 or higher on at least one exam.

### Count Once Or Many Times?

Compare two possible datasets: one with graduates as cases and one with individual AP exams as cases. Ask how the same school could look different depending on which unit is used.

### Ranking Audit

Show students the College Board definition used for state rankings and ask whether it rewards broad access, high scores among a smaller group, or some combination of both.

### Percentage-Point Change

Have students compute and interpret the change from 20.7% in 2015 to 24.8% in 2025, then discuss why saying "up 4.1 percentage points" is clearer than saying "up 19.8%."

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the population behind the 37.0% and 24.8% figures?
2. Why is "took at least one AP exam" not the same as "earned a 3 or higher on at least one AP exam"?
3. If one student takes five AP exams, how many times should that student count in different summaries?
4. Why might state rankings based on AP outcomes reflect access as well as academic performance?
5. What kinds of students or schools might be underrepresented in these summaries?
6. What additional data would you want before making a strong claim that one state is doing a better job than another?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

Explain why the share of graduates who took at least one AP exam is a different statistic from the share who scored 3 or higher on at least one AP exam.

### Concept Check

What is the denominator in the statement "24.8% of U.S. public high school graduates scored a 3 or higher on at least one AP exam"?

### Exit Ticket

Name one reason a state with a high AP success rate might still have unequal access to AP opportunities.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: interpreting a percentage.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: What does it mean to say that 37.0% of U.S. public high school graduates in the class of 2025 took at least one AP exam?
- Answer: It means 37.0% of graduates from that public-school graduating class took one or more AP exams during high school.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: distinguishing variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: Are "took at least one AP exam" and "scored a 3 or higher on at least one AP exam" the same variable?
- Answer: No. One measures participation and the other measures a thresholded performance outcome.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage-point change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: The share scoring 3 or higher rose from 20.7% in 2015 to 24.8% in 2025. What is the percentage-point increase?
- Answer: 4.1 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: denominator choice.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is it important that the College Board percentages use all U.S. public high school graduates as the denominator rather than only AP test takers?
- Answer: Because that denominator reflects both access to AP and outcomes among the full graduating class, not just results among students who already participated.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: representation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Give one reason two states with similar AP success percentages could still differ in the kinds of students getting access to AP.
- Answer: A strong answer notes that similar overall percentages can hide differences in which schools, neighborhoods, or student groups have AP opportunities.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious comparison.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A student says, "State A ranks above State B, so State A definitely has better schools." What is a better response?
- Answer: The ranking may reflect many factors, including access, demographics, course availability, and how students are counted, so it does not by itself prove State A has better schools.

## Teaching Note

This story is especially useful early in the term because many students already know what AP is. That familiarity lets the class focus on statistical reasoning about denominators, summaries, and comparison rather than on learning a new domain.
