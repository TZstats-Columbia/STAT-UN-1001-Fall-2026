## Student-facing paragraph

Does where you exercise change the experience? Researchers compared forest and urban sessions in the same 47 adults. Each session involved 30 minutes of running or walking at a prescribed moderate perceived effort. Participants generally covered more distance and reported greater enjoyment in the forest. They also reported stronger intentions to exercise again. Before turning this into advice for everyone, ask: Why compare each person with themselves? Is wanting to exercise later the same as actually doing it? What other differences between the routes might matter?

## Synopsis and instructor context

The randomized, counterbalanced crossover study recruited healthy adults aged 18–31 in Leicestershire, UK. Most were already physically active. It compared one woodland route with one industrial-area route; their surfaces and elevation changes differed. The paper characterized the distance effect as small. This provides a concrete distinction between evidence about the tested settings and claims about every natural environment or sustained behavior.

For teaching, start with the familiar decision of choosing a route. Students need no exercise-science background. The university article's enthusiasm creates an opportunity to practice constructive headline revision. Keep the lesson about evidence rather than asking students to disclose fitness, health, or body measurements. Use the paper's design as a model for discussing what a comparison can answer, and the original fictional exercise below for arithmetic.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

By the end of a 20–25 minute lesson, students should be able to:

- Identify a participant as a case and distinguish a person from repeated observations of that person.
- Classify setting as categorical, distance as quantitative, and a rating-scale response as a measured report rather than direct behavior.
- Explain how pairing addresses stable differences among people, while randomized order addresses a different issue.
- Interpret an average difference alongside individual variation.
- Separate random assignment from representative sampling and distinguish an immediate outcome from a long-term claim.

These are conceptual learning-outcome alignments, not claims about a particular scheduled course week.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- **Paired observations:** Two measurements on one person belong together. Counting sessions as unrelated people exaggerates the amount of independent information.
- **Comparison and order:** A second attempt might differ through practice or fatigue. Randomizing order helps prevent one setting from consistently receiving that advantage or disadvantage.
- **Measurement:** A stated intention is a variable worth studying, but cannot substitute for subsequent attendance or activity records.
- **Scope of causation:** Assigning settings strengthens a causal comparison of the tested experiences. It does not isolate trees from terrain, noise, expectations, or other components of those experiences.
- **Representation:** Random assignment does not make participants a random sample of all people. Ask what recruitment and eligibility imply for other ages, activity levels, and access needs.
- **Uncertainty:** An average advantage need not occur for everyone. Lack of a statistically detectable difference on another outcome would not prove exact equality.

## Suggestions for class activities

### 1. Build a data dictionary (5 minutes)

In pairs, propose columns for participant ID, session order, setting, distance, enjoyment rating, and later exercise behavior. Mark which columns describe the immediate session and which would require follow-up. Ask students how they would define “exercised again” consistently.

### 2. Preserve the pairs (8 minutes)

**Entirely fictional teaching data, not study results.** Distances are meters in equal-duration sessions.

| Person | Urban distance | Forest distance | Forest minus urban |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 2,000 | 2,300 | 300 |
| B | 2,800 | 2,900 | 100 |
| C | 3,400 | 3,300 | -100 |
| D | 4,000 | 4,200 | 200 |
| E | 4,600 | 4,600 | 0 |
| F | 5,200 | 5,600 | 400 |

Ask students to calculate the mean paired difference, count how many improved, and draw a dotplot of the differences with zero marked. Then sketch connected urban/forest points for each person. Discuss what a chart showing only two averages would conceal.

**Instructor key:** Differences sum to 900 meters; mean difference = 150 meters. Four of six improve, one is unchanged, and one decreases. Urban mean = 3,666.7 meters; forest mean = 3,816.7 meters. The difference in means equals the mean paired difference for these complete pairs, but retaining pairs matters for displaying variation and assessing uncertainty.

### 3. Design the missing follow-up (7 minutes)

Have groups propose a study of whether access to a pleasant route changes activity over a month. Require a defined outcome, comparison, recruitment plan, and plan for missing follow-up observations. Invite walking, wheeling, or other accessible activities in their proposals; no physical participation is required. Ask what time, transport, safety, and access differences could affect both uptake and who remains in the study.

## Discussion questions

1. What personal differences does comparing each participant with themselves help control? What can still change between sessions?
2. Why might testing one forest and one urban route leave uncertainty about a different pair of routes?
3. How could expectations affect an enjoyment rating when participants can see the setting?
4. What evidence would be necessary for a claim about exercise habits six months later?
5. Does a positive average imply that everyone benefited? Use the fictional table to explain.
6. How could researchers include people with different mobility needs or limited access to green space without assuming that one activity suits everyone?

## Example assessment instruments

### Multiple-choice concept check (1 point)

Which feature most directly reduces the influence of stable differences in baseline fitness on the setting comparison?

A. Calling the result statistically significant.  
B. Having each participant experience both settings.  
C. Measuring only intentions.  
D. Recruiting from one region.

**Key:** B. Pairing supplies a comparison within each person.

### Numerical and graphical task (4 points)

Using the fictional table, report the mean forest-minus-urban difference, the fraction with a positive difference, and one reason to show individual differences rather than only means.

**Key/rubric:** 150 meters (1 point); 4/6 or about 66.7% (1 point); recognizes differing individual responses (1 point) and explains that a positive mean does not imply universal improvement (1 point).

### Critique a claim (4 points)

A hypothetical headline says, “Forest routes make everyone maintain exercise for a year.” Identify two unsupported extensions and describe one additional measurement or design feature that would address each.

**Key/rubric:** Universal population claim exceeds the recruited sample/tested settings (1 point); proposes broader recruitment and multiple routes (1 point). A year of behavior was not established by immediate intentions (1 point); proposes prospective behavioral follow-up with a comparison and attention to missing observations (1 point).

### Exit ticket (2 points)

Explain in one sentence why randomizing session order and randomly sampling people solve different problems.

**Key/rubric:** Order randomization helps prevent order effects from systematically favoring one setting (1 point); probability sampling supports inference to a defined population (1 point).
