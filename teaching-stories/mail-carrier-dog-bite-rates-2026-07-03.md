## Accessible Student Source

Use the Axios Pittsburgh piece together with the USPS release. Axios gives students an easy entry through a city-level comparison, and the USPS release provides the broader national counts behind the story.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

The U.S. Postal Service reported more than 5,200 dog attacks on postal workers in 2025 and listed the cities with the most incidents. Los Angeles had the most reported incidents with 70, while Axios highlighted that Pittsburgh's 21 incidents translated to nearly 7 incidents per 100,000 residents and that Dayton's 19 incidents translated to an even higher rate of 13.8 per 100,000 residents. This is a strong statistics story because raw counts and rates tell different stories, and students can ask whether population is even the best denominator for comparing risk to mail carriers.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is excellent for introductory statistics because the numbers are simple but the interpretation is not. Students can immediately compare counts such as 70 incidents in Los Angeles and 19 in Dayton, then see how rankings change when those counts are converted into rates. That makes the story an accessible introduction to why context and denominators matter.

It also opens a deeper conversation about measurement and representation. Incidents per 100,000 residents may be more informative than raw counts for some purposes, but residents are not the people being bitten. Mail carrier routes, delivery density, dog ownership, reporting practices, and urban form may all affect the comparison. This lets students discuss whether a variable is a good proxy, when a ranking can mislead, and how a better data collection design might change the analysis.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: cities, states, or incident reports.
- Variables: number of incidents, rate per residents, city size, route density, and reporting practices.
- Counts versus rates.
- Choice of denominator.
- Proxy measures.
- Confounding and lurking variables.
- Ranking instability.
- Representation and comparability.
- Cautious interpretation of descriptive data.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Count Versus Rate Ranking

Give students the reported counts for several cities and ask them to rank the cities by count and then by rate. Compare how the rankings change.

### Is Population The Right Denominator?

Ask students to brainstorm alternative denominators such as number of carriers, delivery addresses, route miles, or dog-owning households, then discuss which would better match the question of worker risk.

### Build A Better Graph

Students redesign a headline or graph so that it does not imply that the city with the largest count automatically has the highest risk.

### Small Numbers And Uncertainty

Use a simple simulation or repeated-sampling discussion to show how rankings based on modest annual counts can move around even without major underlying changes.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why can raw counts and rates lead to different conclusions about which city has the biggest problem?
2. What does "incidents per 100,000 residents" measure well, and what does it measure poorly?
3. Why might population be an imperfect denominator for mail carrier risk?
4. What lurking variables could help explain differences across cities?
5. If a city moves up or down in the rankings next year, would that automatically mean the underlying danger changed a lot?
6. What additional data would help make a fairer comparison across cities?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: counts versus rates.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Los Angeles had 70 incidents and Dayton had 19 incidents, yet Dayton had a higher incident rate. What explains how both statements can be true?
- Answer: Los Angeles had more total incidents, but Dayton has a much smaller population, so its incidents per resident were higher.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: denominator choice.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: multiple choice.
- Prompt: Which denominator would best match the question "How much risk do mail carriers face?" A. City population B. Number of mail carriers or delivery routes C. Number of parks
- Answer: B. Number of mail carriers or delivery routes.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: rate calculation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: Suppose a city with 400,000 residents had 20 incidents. What is the incident rate per 100,000 residents?
- Answer: 5 incidents per 100,000 residents.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: lurking variables.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Give one lurking variable that could affect comparisons of dog-bite incidents across cities.
- Answer: A strong answer could name number of carriers, number of delivery stops, dog ownership rates, or reporting practices.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: ranking instability.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: If one city has 20 incidents and another has 21 in one year, why should we be cautious about treating the rankings as a big difference?
- Answer: The counts are very close, so ordinary year-to-year variability could easily change the order without reflecting a major real difference.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better study design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a measure.
- Prompt: Propose a better metric than incidents per 100,000 residents for comparing the risk faced by mail carriers across cities.
- Answer: A strong answer uses a direct exposure measure such as incidents per carrier, per route, or per delivery stop.
