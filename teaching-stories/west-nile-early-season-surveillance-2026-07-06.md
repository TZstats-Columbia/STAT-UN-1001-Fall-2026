## Accessible Student Source

Use the AP News story. It is short, concrete, and readable for students without a health background, while the CDC pages provide the surveillance context and limitations that make the statistics discussion much richer.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

As of June 30, 2026, the CDC had confirmed at least 48 West Nile virus cases in the United States, including 38 severe cases, and AP reported that this was the worst start to the season in more than 20 years. At first glance, that sounds like a simple trend story, but it raises several classic statistics questions. Are we comparing raw counts or comparable rates? Are mild cases undercounted more than severe ones? Does a larger number of reported states mean more disease, better detection, or both? This makes a strong introductory statistics case because students can analyze measurement limits, underreporting, and how headlines turn surveillance data into claims.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story is excellent for *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because the numbers are immediately understandable but the measurement process is not. Students can start with the headline comparison between this year's early-season count and past years, then move quickly into the harder question of what surveillance data actually measure. The CDC explicitly notes that the data are preliminary, that reporting lags matter, and that mild cases are more likely to be missed than severe cases.

That combination supports several core course goals. Students can distinguish cases from variables, counts from rates, and reported disease from true disease burden. They can also discuss why an apparent increase in reports does not automatically imply an equally large increase in underlying infections. This is a clean way to introduce underreporting, selection effects, changing detection, and cautious interpretation without requiring advanced medical knowledge.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the claim behind "worst start in more than 20 years."
- Week 2: Define the cases and variables in a disease surveillance system.
- Week 3: Distinguish population quantity from reported sample or surveillance counts.
- Week 4: Compare raw counts with more informative rates or proportions.
- Week 5: Interpret the difference between total cases and severe cases.
- Week 6: Discuss why reported counts vary because of both chance and detection.
- Week 7: Introduce undercoverage and reporting bias.
- Week 8: Frame questions about whether the season is truly worse or only more visible.
- Week 9: Practice cautious causal reasoning when weather, mosquito exposure, and reporting all change together.
- Week 10: Use the story to discuss uncertainty in public-health monitoring data.
- Week 11: Examine operational definitions such as "case," "severe case," and "state with detection."
- Week 12: Revisit how data limitations affect public communication.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: reported human West Nile cases.
- Variables: date reported, severity, state, county of residence, and outcome.
- Counts versus rates.
- Proportions within a total.
- Underreporting.
- Detection bias and reporting lag.
- Selection effects in surveillance systems.
- Comparability across time and place.
- Cautious interpretation of trend claims.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Headline Audit

Ask students what evidence is needed to support the phrase "worst start." Should the comparison use raw case counts, severe-case counts, rates per population, or something else?

### Severe Versus Mild Cases

Have students compute the share of reported cases that were severe and then discuss why that proportion may not match the share of all infections that are severe.

### What Gets Missed?

Use the CDC limitations section to identify at least three reasons surveillance counts can differ from true disease burden.

### Design A Better Comparison

Students propose a fairer year-to-year comparison, such as severe cases by date, cases per population, or a time series using the same calendar cutoff.

## Discussion Questions

1. What exactly is being counted in this story: infections, diagnosed cases, or reported cases?
2. Why might mild cases be underrepresented compared with severe cases?
3. What does it mean to say that 23 states have detected West Nile activity? Does that measure spread, surveillance effort, or both?
4. Why should we be cautious about comparing early-season counts across years?
5. What denominator would you want if you were comparing Arizona with another state?
6. What additional data would help you decide whether 2026 is truly a worse season or just an earlier-detected one?

## Example Assessment Instruments

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: data source interpretation.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: In this story, are the 48 cases best described as all infections in the country or reported confirmed cases in a surveillance system?
- Answer: Reported confirmed cases in a surveillance system.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: proportion calculation.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: If 38 of 48 reported cases were severe, what proportion of the reported cases were severe?
- Answer: 38/48, or about 79%.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: underreporting.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Why might the 79% severe share among reported cases overstate the severe share among all infections?
- Answer: Mild cases are less likely to be diagnosed and reported, so severe cases make up a larger share of the reported dataset than of all infections.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: counts versus rates.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is it often better to compare disease rates than raw case counts across states?
- Answer: States have different population sizes, so equal or unequal counts may not reflect equal or unequal risk.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: measurement limitations.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: The CDC says cases are reported by county of residence, not county of exposure. Why does that matter?
- Answer: A person may be bitten and infected in a different place from where they live, so residence does not always identify where exposure happened.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better study design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a comparison.
- Prompt: Propose one better statistical comparison for evaluating whether the 2026 season is unusually severe by early July.
- Answer: A strong answer compares the same calendar-date cutoff across years using severe cases or rates per population, while noting consistent reporting rules.

## Teaching Note

This story is especially useful early in the term because students can understand the public-health context right away, yet the data production process is complicated enough to show why "the number in the headline" is rarely the whole story.
