## Accessible Student Source

Use the main Axios Richmond story. It is short and readable, and it links the public debate about data centers to a broader drought context that students can understand without technical background.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

During a severe 2026 drought in the Richmond area, local officials asked residents to conserve water, and public attention quickly turned to data centers. But the reporting also noted that in Henrico County, apartment complexes and hospitals were larger water users than data centers. This is a strong statistics story because it asks students to think carefully about what is being measured, what comparisons are fair, and how public attention can focus on one category even when the data tell a more complicated story. It is especially good for discussing categories, proportions, proxies, and the difference between anecdote and systematic evidence.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it turns a familiar public argument into a clean measurement problem. Students can begin with the broad claim that data centers are draining local water supplies, then ask what evidence would be needed to evaluate that claim. Are we comparing total water use, peak-period use, seasonal use, water per facility, or water per dollar of economic activity? Each choice changes the story.

The Richmond drought rules make the context even stronger. The city triggered voluntary conservation measures beginning July 1, 2026, when average James River flows dropped to 1,700 cubic feet per second for 14 consecutive days. That lets students separate at least three related but distinct questions: how drought is measured, how restrictions are triggered, and how water use is distributed across categories of users. The story supports reasoning about denominators, category summaries, salience bias, and the need for better data before strong causal claims are made.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### What Would You Measure?

Ask students to list at least four different ways to measure whether data centers are a "big" water user. Compare how each measure could change the conclusion.

### Threshold Trigger

Use the Richmond rule that voluntary conservation starts when average flow drops to 1,700 cfs for 14 straight days. Ask students why policymakers might use a threshold and what information is lost when a continuous variable becomes a yes/no trigger.

### Category Share Debate

Give students a hypothetical table of local water use by category and ask them whether the phrase "largest user" is enough to answer public concerns about drought.

### Public Attention Versus Data

Have students discuss why one category, such as data centers, can dominate headlines even if other categories use more water overall.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main statistical claim people seem to be making when they blame data centers for drought stress?
2. Why might total water use and water use during drought periods tell different stories?
3. What does the Richmond drought trigger measure, and what does it not measure?
4. Why is it risky to compare categories of users without knowing how many facilities are in each category?
5. How can public attention create a biased picture of which users matter most?
6. What additional data would help you evaluate whether data centers deserve more or less concern than other water users?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: identifying a variable.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: In Richmond's drought plan, what is the measured variable used to trigger voluntary conservation?
- Answer: Average James River flow, measured in cubic feet per second.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: threshold rules.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: What happens under Richmond's plan when average flows stay below 1,700 cfs for 14 consecutive days?
- Answer: Voluntary water conservation measures are implemented.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: category comparison.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is it not enough to know that one category uses more total water than another?
- Answer: Because totals ignore context such as how many users are in each category, when the water is used, and what question the comparison is meant to answer.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: salience bias.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Give one reason the public might focus heavily on data centers even if another category uses more water.
- Answer: A strong answer notes that data centers are a visible, controversial, fast-growing industry, so they attract attention more easily than familiar categories like apartments or hospitals.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: better study design.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: design.
- Prompt: Suggest one additional piece of data that would improve the comparison across water-user categories.
- Answer: A strong answer could suggest number of facilities, seasonal water use, peak-day water use, or water use per facility.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: cautious causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: critique.
- Prompt: A headline says, "Drought proves data centers are unsustainable." What is one statistical reason to be cautious about that claim?
- Answer: The drought indicator and the category-use data do not by themselves show that data centers are the main cause of the drought or that their role is larger than that of other users.

## Teaching Note

This story is very usable because students can understand the public argument immediately, but the underlying statistical questions are richer than they first appear. It is particularly good for showing how data collection choices shape debates about fairness and responsibility.
