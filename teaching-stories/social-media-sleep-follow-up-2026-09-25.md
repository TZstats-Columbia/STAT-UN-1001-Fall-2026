# Social media, sleep, and what “predicts” means

## Student-facing paragraph

Does time on social media tell us something about later sleep? Researchers followed 349 first-year George Mason students, ages 18–24, using surveys across three semesters. Greater total social media use was associated with poorer sleep, including at a later assessment. Sleep was also related to how students rated their health. These were observations, not assigned phone habits. What could the word “predicts” mean here, and what more would we need before claiming that reducing social media improves health? [Study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42631550/)

## Synopsis and instructor context

The university describes surveys in August 2019, February 2020, and August 2020, and notes self-report and participant drop-off. The pandemic crossed the observation window. This makes an accessible case for discussing time order without treating it as proof of causation. No platform-specific knowledge is needed. Unlike the collection’s nap/exercise experiment, this story concerns observational follow-up and measurement.

The abstract reports correlations of .21 cross-sectionally and .22 longitudinally between total use and poor sleep quality. Nighttime use was not associated with sleep quality or self-rated health. The authors discuss sleep as a possible pathway; students need not learn mediation analysis. Do not interpret .22 as a 22% health deterioration or a percentage of students affected. [Research abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42631550/)

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students practice identifying the evidence behind a claim, defining cases and variables, distinguishing observational studies from experiments, evaluating representation, and writing conclusions with appropriate scope. Fits an introductory class on association and cautious causal reasoning; no health-science background is required.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- **Cases and repeated measurements:** A student is a case; multiple surveys from that student do not create independent new people.
- **Measurement:** Perceived health differs from a diagnosis; recalled time may differ from device-recorded time.
- **Sampling and attrition:** Ask who enrolled and who remained, before generalizing to all college students.
- **Confounding:** Work hours, stress, housing, or prior health are possible alternative explanations to investigate, not established explanations of this result.
- **Uncertainty:** A reported absence of association does not establish an exactly zero effect.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Evidence timeline (8 minutes):** Draw three survey occasions. Add a hypothetical stress variable that could influence both phone use and sleep. Label which arrows are hypotheses rather than findings. Discuss why later measurement helps but does not create random assignment.
2. **Measurement workshop (8 minutes):** Groups propose a question for total use, sleep quality, and perceived health. Compare recall periods, units, and ambiguous terms. Use invented responses, so personal disclosure is unnecessary.
3. **Attrition arithmetic (8 minutes):** Entirely fictional example: Of 100 invited students, 60 respond and 24 report poor sleep. Compute 24/60 = 40%. If all 40 nonrespondents had good sleep, the invited-group proportion would be 24%; if all had poor sleep, 64%. These are extreme missing-data bounds, not confidence intervals or study estimates.

## Discussion questions

- How could a student’s paid work schedule affect both measured variables?
- Which population would you want represented before applying the claim to commuter, residential, and older students?
- What would a device log improve, and what would it still fail to measure?
- What extra information would help distinguish “no evidence of a relationship” from “evidence of little relationship”?

## Example assessment instruments

**Multiple choice (1 point):** Following students over time automatically makes this study (A) randomized, (B) a census, (C) longitudinal observational, or (D) free of confounding. **Key: C.**

**Short answer (4 points):** Give two reasons not to conclude that reducing use must improve every student’s health. **Key/rubric:** 1 point each for two relevant limitations, plus 1 each for explaining their consequences. Examples: unassigned exposure leaves possible confounding; one-campus participants may not represent other students; self-report may mismeasure variables.

**Numerical exit ticket (3 points):** In the fictional activity, explain why 40% is not necessarily the invited-group percentage. **Key:** It describes respondents (1); nonrespondents may differ (1); 24%–64% are logically possible under the stated assumptions (1).

**Claim revision (2 points):** Rewrite “Social media causes poor health.” **Example:** “In this student sample, greater reported social media use was associated with poorer sleep; the observational evidence does not by itself establish a causal health effect.” Award 1 for bounded population/outcome and 1 for association language.
