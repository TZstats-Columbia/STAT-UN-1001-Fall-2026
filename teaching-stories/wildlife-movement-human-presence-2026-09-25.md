## Student-facing paragraph

A park can have the same trails but very different numbers of visitors. Researchers compared GPS records from 4,581 mammals and birds representing 37 species with neighborhood-level, anonymized cellphone location data. They examined matching periods in 2019 and 2020, when human movement changed during the pandemic. Wildlife responses varied across species and landscapes. How well do roads, phones, and GPS tags measure the things we really want to know? ## Synopsis and instructor context

The study separates two ideas students might conflate: alteration of a landscape and people’s physical presence. The university report describes weekly animal tracking and explains that movement changes alone do not establish whether animals benefit or experience stress. Its everyday hook is sharing outdoor spaces with wildlife. This differs from the existing shark-detection story: focus here is competing measurements of exposure, repeated records, and comparisons across time.

Keep ecological modeling optional. The scientific question can be translated into “Which animals, where, when, and compared with what?” Avoid calling pandemic restrictions a randomized experiment. ## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students identify cases and variables, evaluate proxies and representation, compare groups and time periods, choose graphs, and separate observed behavior from claims about its consequences. Suitable for an introductory measurement or observational-study lesson without ecology prerequisites.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- **Operational definitions:** Built area, visitor counts, and phone-location records measure different aspects of human activity.
- **Unit of analysis:** A species, animal, and animal-week are distinct units; many GPS points are not many independent animals.
- **Representation:** Consider which species and locations can be tagged and which visitors appear in a phone dataset.
- **Comparison:** Matching seasons helps address seasonality but does not rule out other year-to-year changes.
- **Association and uncertainty:** A change in space use is not automatically a change in welfare or survival.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Proxy audit (10 minutes):** Compare three proposed park measures: paved area, entrance-counter readings, and phone records. For each, name what it measures well, what it may miss, and one way to validate it. Possible issues to investigate include repeated entrances, visitors without recorded phones, and roads with few current users.
2. **Graph workshop (10 minutes):** Use the wholly fictional table below. Plot paired points for each park’s visitor count in 2019 and 2020. Then plot visitor change against animal-area change. Describe the pattern without claiming cause. Animal area is an invented average weekly area used, not a study estimate.

| Park | Daily visitors 2019 | Daily visitors 2020 | Animal area 2019 (km²) | Animal area 2020 (km²) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 100 | 50 | 2.0 | 3.0 |
| B | 200 | 180 | 2.0 | 2.2 |
| C | 80 | 120 | 2.0 | 1.5 |

**Instructor key:** Visitor changes are −50, −20, +40; area changes are +1.0, +0.2, −0.5 km². These three invented parks show a negative association between changes. They cannot establish a general wildlife pattern or a causal effect.

3. **Design improvement (5 minutes):** Ask groups to add one comparison or measurement. Examples include comparable parks, weather information, or consistent tracking schedules. Require an explanation of the uncertainty addressed and one that remains.

## Discussion questions

- Could the same amount of pavement support very different visitor numbers?
- Would tracking more points from one animal solve poor representation of species?
- What besides visitors might change between two years?
- What additional outcome would help determine whether a movement change is beneficial?
- Why might one average across species conceal useful differences?

## Example assessment instruments

**Multiple choice (1 point):** Ten thousand GPS readings from ten animals necessarily provide (A) 10,000 independent animals, (B) ten animals with repeated measurements, or (C) a random sample of all wildlife. **Key: B.**

**Calculation (3 points):** For fictional Park A, calculate the visitor percentage change and animal-area percentage change. **Key:** −50% and +50% (1 point each); the matching magnitudes do not prove causation (1).

**Measurement critique (4 points):** A researcher labels road density “number of people present.” Explain a problem and propose validation. **Key/rubric:** distinguish infrastructure from current presence (1); give an example of mismatch (1); propose direct counts at matched places/times (1); identify a remaining limitation such as coverage of counting locations (1).

**Headline revision (2 points):** Revise “Lockdowns made all wildlife healthier.” **Example:** “Tracking data revealed differing wildlife movement responses associated with human presence; movement alone does not show improved health.” Award 1 for avoiding universal/causal wording and 1 for distinguishing movement from health.
