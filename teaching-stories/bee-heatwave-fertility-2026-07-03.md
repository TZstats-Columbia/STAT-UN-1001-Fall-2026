## Accessible Student Source

Use the Guardian article. It is short, concrete, and accessible without requiring biology background, while still giving students clear numerical comparisons between the heatwave and control groups.

## Student-Facing Paragraph

Researchers simulated a three-day heatwave for red mason bees and compared those bees with a control group kept in ordinary summer conditions. Months later, male bees from the heatwave group had about half the sperm activity of the control group, and females showed about a 15% reduction in the size and number of developing eggs. This is a strong statistics story because it gives students a simple comparison with measurable outcomes and raises good questions about variation, uncertainty, and how far results from one experiment can be generalized to other bees or other environments.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story works well in *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because it looks simple at first and then opens into several foundational questions. Students can identify the explanatory variable (heatwave versus ordinary conditions) and response variables (sperm activity, egg development), then ask how convincing the comparison is and what kinds of conclusions are justified.

The story is especially useful for distinguishing a controlled experiment from an observational study. Because the researchers created the heatwave conditions in the lab, students can discuss why that strengthens causal claims about heat exposure and fertility within the experiment. At the same time, the story naturally invites caution about external validity: one bee species, one experimental setup, and one heatwave scenario do not automatically represent all bee populations or all climate conditions. That combination makes the story a clean entry point for comparison, variation, and cautious causal reasoning.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Identify The Design

Ask students to classify the study as experimental or observational and justify their answer using the description of how the heatwave conditions were created.

### Translate The Percentages

Have students restate the reported 50% drop and 15% reduction in plain language, then discuss what additional numerical details they would want before deciding how large the effect really is.

### Sketch Possible Data

Students create simple dotplots or side-by-side boxplots for hypothetical sperm-activity or egg-count data that would match the story's summary.

### Generalization Debate

Split the class into two groups: one argues that the study is strong evidence that heatwaves threaten pollination, while the other argues that more data are needed before making broad claims.

## Discussion Questions

1. What makes this study closer to a controlled experiment than to an observational study?
2. What exactly was compared between the heatwave and control groups?
3. Why is a reported percentage drop not enough by itself to tell us everything about the data?
4. What kinds of natural variation might still exist even if heat truly affects bee fertility?
5. How far should we generalize from one bee species in one laboratory setting?
6. What additional information would make you more confident in the broader ecological claim?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: study design.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: Researchers created heatwave conditions for one group of bees and ordinary summer conditions for another group. Is this an experiment or an observational study?
- Answer: Experiment.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: variables.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: identify variables.
- Prompt: In this story, which is the explanatory variable: sperm activity or heatwave condition?
- Answer: Heatwave condition.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: If the control group's average sperm activity were indexed at 100 units, what would a 50% drop imply for the heatwave group's average?
- Answer: 50 units.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: cautious generalization.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Give one reason the experiment does not automatically prove that all bee species will respond the same way to heatwaves.
- Answer: The study used one species in one laboratory setup, so results may differ for other species or field conditions.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Why does the study design support a causal claim about heat exposure within the experiment?
- Answer: Because the researchers actively assigned the heatwave versus control conditions, making the treatment the main planned difference between groups.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: uncertainty and replication.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a follow-up.
- Prompt: Propose one follow-up study that would strengthen the evidence for a broader claim about climate change and pollinators.
- Answer: A strong answer suggests replication across multiple bee species, larger samples, or more realistic field settings.
