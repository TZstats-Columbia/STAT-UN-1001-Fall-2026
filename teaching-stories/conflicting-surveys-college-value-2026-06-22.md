## Student-Facing Paragraph

Different surveys seem to tell different stories about higher education. One UK survey reported that the share of people who think a degree is not worth the time and money rose from 14% in 2005 to 34% in 2025. But a U.S. AP-NORC poll found that about 6 in 10 teenagers said graduating from college was extremely or very important to them, and a Gallup poll found that public confidence in higher education rose in 2025 from recent lows. This is a useful statistics case because the surveys are not necessarily contradicting each other. They asked different people, in different places, at different times, using different questions.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This case study is built around an apparent conflict: one report suggests declining belief in the value of university, while others suggest college still matters and may even be regaining public confidence. The key statistical lesson is that survey results depend heavily on population, wording, timing, and the exact outcome being measured.

The UK survey asks whether a degree is "worth the time and money," which invites respondents to weigh costs, debt, and labor-market payoff. The AP-NORC teen survey asks whether graduating from college is important to teenagers' goals, which is closer to aspiration or perceived importance. The Gallup confidence survey asks about trust in institutions, which is a different concept again. These stories look contradictory only if readers collapse different survey questions into a single vague idea of "what people think about education."

This fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* especially well because it teaches students to slow down before comparing percentages across studies. It is a strong example of operational definitions, target population, framing, trend interpretation, subgroup emphasis, and why two true survey results can still support very different headlines.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 1: Identify the claim and ask what each survey is actually measuring.
- Week 2: Discuss population, sample, wording, and representativeness.
- Week 3: Compare percentages carefully across surveys and across time.
- Week 4: Practice distinguishing substantive disagreement from measurement differences.
- Week 5: Use the story to discuss conditional statements about subgroups and how risk or value claims shift across populations.
- Week 6: Treat survey proportions as sample-based random quantities that vary across repeated polls.
- Week 7: Discuss confidence intervals and whether apparently different survey estimates might still overlap substantially.
- Week 8: Frame testable questions about whether changes over time or differences across groups are larger than sampling variability.
- Week 9: Distinguish statistical significance from headline-friendly narratives of "confidence crisis" or "college comeback."
- Week 10: Use the story for inference with proportions and comparisons across categorical groups.
- Week 11: Discuss when numerical outcomes such as earnings would answer a different question than opinion data.
- Week 12: Revisit the story as a capstone on measurement, comparability, generalization, and communication.
- Textbook fit: OpenIntro Ch. 1-2 for surveys and variables; Ch. 5-7 for uncertainty and categorical comparisons.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: survey respondents.
- Variables: perceived worth, importance of graduating, confidence in higher education, age, country, and year.
- Operational definition.
- Population versus sample.
- Question wording effects.
- Time effects versus age effects.
- Cross-survey comparability.
- Media framing and selective emphasis.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Same Topic, Different Variable

Ask students to list what each survey is measuring in one sentence. Then ask whether those measures should be treated as interchangeable.

### Population Check

Make a three-column table for the UK public, U.S. teens, and U.S. adults. Ask students what comparisons are fair and which ones are misleading.

### Headline Rewrite

Give students the raw summaries and ask them to write two different honest headlines: one skeptical about college and one optimistic about college. Then discuss how framing changes interpretation.

### Bias Hunt

Have students identify possible sources of bias or distortion in each survey story: wording effects, age differences, national context, nonresponse, and journalistic emphasis on a surprising subgroup.

## Discussion Questions

1. Do these surveys actually contradict one another, or do they measure different things?
2. What are the cases and variables in each survey?
3. Why is it risky to compare U.K. adults in 2025 directly with U.S. teenagers in 2025 or U.S. adults in 2022?
4. How does the phrase "worth the time and money" differ from "important for life goals"?
5. How can two accurate surveys still produce headlines that feel inconsistent?
6. What additional data would help us connect public opinion about college with actual educational or labor-market outcomes?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

One survey says more people think a degree is not worth the time and money, while another says most teens think graduating from college is important. Give two reasons these findings may both be true at the same time.

### Comparison Prompt

For each survey, identify the target population and the main response variable. Then explain why comparing the percentages directly may be misleading.

### Exit Ticket

What is one example of a wording difference that could change how respondents answer questions about higher education?

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies that the surveys measure different constructs.
- Recognizes differences in population and timing.
- Uses survey language accurately.
- Avoids treating all education-attitude percentages as directly comparable.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: operational definition.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: One survey asks whether a degree is "worth the time and money," while another asks whether graduating from college is important. What key statistical idea explains why these may not be directly comparable?
- Answer: They use different operational definitions or response variables.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: survey comparability.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If two surveys report percentages about college in the same year, their results can be directly compared even if they ask different questions to different populations.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percentage-point change.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: The UK share saying a degree is not worth the time and money rose from 14% in 2005 to 34% in 2025. What is the percentage-point increase?
- Answer: 34% - 14% = 20 percentage points.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpretation across surveys.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Give one reason the UK adult survey and the U.S. teen poll can both be accurate without actually contradicting each other.
- Answer: They measure different populations and different questions, so they do not estimate the same underlying quantity.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: wording effects.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: How might responses change if a survey asked whether college is "financially worth it" instead of "worth the time and money"?
- Answer: A strong answer notes that the wording shifts attention toward earnings and away from broader goals, which could change responses.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: stronger survey comparison design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Design one better cross-national survey comparison about college value. State the target populations, one common question, and one step you would take to improve comparability.
- Answer: A strong answer uses matched populations, harmonized wording, and comparable timing or sampling procedures.

## Teaching Note

If you want a sharper classroom contrast, pair only the UK story and the AP-NORC teen poll. If you want a fuller methods discussion, add the Gallup confidence story as a third measure and ask students to map all three onto different underlying concepts: value, aspiration, and institutional trust.
