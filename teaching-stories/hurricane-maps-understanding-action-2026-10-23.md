# Does a clearer hurricane map change what people do?

Curated: October 23, 2026. New to this collection; fresh October 20 coverage of research described by FSU on August 11, not a newly conducted October study.

## Student-facing paragraph

A hurricane map can be easier to read without changing someone's plans. Florida State University researchers compared traditional forecast maps with maps adding inland watches and warnings. Participants in Florida and Georgia who saw the revised maps identified watch areas better, but the report did not find corresponding improvements in perceived risk or intended protective action. How should we measure whether a map “works”? Identifying a location, feeling concerned, planning to act, and actually acting are different outcomes.

## Synopsis / instructor context

This is a story about measuring communication success. The university report also describes confusion between cone width and storm size. Use its side-by-side graphics to ask what a visual represents before judging its effectiveness. The study concerns responses to graphics; do not describe it as evidence of actual evacuation behavior or assume random assignment from the news account. No demonstrated difference is not proof of identical effects.

This complements existing weather-probability stories by focusing on comprehension and outcome definitions. No matching map-comprehension story was found in the collection.

## Introduction to Statistical Reasoning alignment

Students translate a broad claim into measurable variables, distinguish a study sample from a target population, compare proportions, and qualify conclusions. No meteorology background is needed: begin with the map legends and distinguish a watch from a warning using the source's labels.

## Statistical concepts illuminated

- Cases: surveyed participants; variables: graphic viewed, identification accuracy, perceived risk, and intended action.
- Measurement: a quiz response, a rating, and a stated intention measure different things.
- Representation: evidence from two states need not describe every audience or language community.
- Comparison and uncertainty: a numerical difference needs context about sample size and variability.
- Causal reasoning: verify assignment and other design details before attributing differences to map design.

## Suggestions for class activities

1. **Map reading (5 minutes):** Show the two FSU graphics. Ask students to explain each layer using the legend and identify what additional information the revised map supplies. Discuss how a shaded region could be misread.
2. **Define success (5 minutes):** Groups propose one comprehension measure and one behavioral measure. Specify when and how each would be recorded. Discuss transportation, work obligations, and access to information as possible barriers between understanding and action; these are discussion hypotheses, not study findings.
3. **Compare outcomes (10 minutes):** Use the fictional counts below, not the study's data. Assume different participants were randomly assigned to the two graphics for this exercise only.

| Fictional outcome | Traditional map (100 people) | Revised map (100 people) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Correctly identify watch area | 55 | 75 |
| Say they would take protective action | 60 | 62 |

Students draw two paired bar comparisons on the same 0–100% scale. Accuracy differs by 20 percentage points; intended action differs by 2 points. These outcomes can overlap within people: do not add the rows. Ask what additional evidence would support a claim about actual behavior. Optional: shuffle 200 cards containing 117 yes responses into two groups of 100 to explore chance differences in the intention outcome.

## Discussion questions

- What does “effective map” mean, and who chooses the outcome?
- Why might comprehension improve without a change in intended action?
- What recruitment information would help judge representation?
- Would randomly assigning maps make the participants a random sample of all residents?
- What information is missing before interpreting a small observed difference?

## Example assessment instruments

**Exit ticket (3 points):** Name the case, an explanatory variable, and one response variable. **Key:** participant; graphic type; identification accuracy, risk rating, or intended action (1 point each).

**Calculation (3 points):** Using the fictional table, calculate the accuracy difference in percentage points and the relative increase over the traditional group. **Key:** 75% − 55% = 20 percentage points; 20/55 ≈ 36.4% relative increase. Award 1 point for each calculation and 1 for distinguishing units.

**Critique (4 points):** Evaluate “The revised map makes everyone safer because more people understand it.” **Key:** identify overgeneralization beyond the sample; distinguish comprehension from safety/behavior; request design evidence for causality; request uncertainty or effect-size information (1 point each).

**Design prompt (2 points):** Explain why random assignment and random sampling answer different questions. **Key:** assignment supports comparability for a causal contrast; sampling supports population representation. Neither automatically supplies the other.
