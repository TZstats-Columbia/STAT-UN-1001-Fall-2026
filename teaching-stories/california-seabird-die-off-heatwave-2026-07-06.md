## Student-Facing Paragraph

Scientists and volunteers along the California coast have been finding unusually large numbers of dead or starving seabirds during a prolonged marine heatwave. The basic story sounds simple: warmer water disrupted the food web, seabirds had less to eat, and more birds died. But the statistics questions are more interesting. What counts as evidence that deaths are unusually high? If most birds die at sea and never wash ashore, how much undercounting is built into the data? Could more observers or more media attention also increase the number of birds found? This is a good statistics story because students can study proxies, missing data, trend comparisons, and how ecological claims are built from incomplete observation.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because it connects a visible real-world event to hidden measurement challenges. The AP story describes decades of beach surveys that establish a baseline, which gives students a concrete way to discuss how comparison works in science. At the same time, the story also notes that only a fraction of birds that die at sea ever reach beaches, which makes the observed count a proxy rather than a full census.

That tension is pedagogically useful. Students can examine how scientists use incomplete data without pretending the data are perfect. They can ask whether a rise in beached birds reflects a rise in total deaths, changes in observation effort, changes in ocean currents, or all three. The story also supports cautious causal reasoning: the marine heatwave is a plausible driver, but several linked steps separate warm water from the final beach counts students see.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Proxy Or Population?

Ask students to separate the observed variable "dead birds found on beaches" from the target quantity "all birds that died because of the event."

### Build The Causal Chain

Have students map the sequence from warm water to lower nutrient upwelling to less prey to starving seabirds to more carcasses on beaches, then discuss where evidence is strongest and weakest.

### What Could Change The Counts?

Students brainstorm alternative reasons beach counts might rise, including stronger survey effort, storms, or shifting currents that move more carcasses ashore.

### Better Graph, Better Claim

Students sketch a graph that compares monthly counts with a historical baseline and includes uncertainty or caveats rather than a single dramatic count.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the difference between the number of birds found and the number of birds that actually died?
2. Why is a long-running baseline important in this story?
3. What makes "birds found on beaches" a proxy measure?
4. How could observation effort affect the counts even if true mortality stayed the same?
5. What evidence would strengthen the claim that the marine heatwave is driving the die-off?
6. Why should we avoid treating a striking local observation as a complete measure of the whole coastal ecosystem?

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: proxy measurement.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: classification.
- Prompt: Are beach carcass counts a direct census of all seabird deaths or a proxy measure?
- Answer: A proxy measure.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: missing data.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Why does the fact that many birds die at sea matter statistically?
- Answer: It means the observed counts likely miss many deaths, so the data undercount the total mortality.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: comparison.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Why is it helpful that some surveys have been conducted for decades?
- Answer: Long-term surveys provide a baseline for judging whether current counts are unusually high relative to past conditions.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: explanation.
- Prompt: Give one reason beach counts could rise even if true seabird deaths did not rise by the same amount.
- Answer: A strong answer could mention more search effort, weather pushing more carcasses ashore, or heightened public reporting.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: causal reasoning.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpretation.
- Prompt: Why is "marine heatwave caused the die-off" a stronger claim than "marine heatwave and die-off happened at the same time"?
- Answer: The causal claim requires evidence that the heatwave changed food availability and mortality, not just that the two events coincided.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: study improvement.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a follow-up.
- Prompt: Propose one additional dataset that would help test the seabird die-off explanation.
- Answer: A strong answer suggests repeated prey surveys, ocean temperature time series, standardized search effort records, or counts from multiple coastal sites over time.

## Teaching Note

This story is a strong bridge from simple counting to deeper reasoning about what observed data stand for, especially when the quantity students really care about cannot be fully measured.
