## Student-Facing Paragraph

San Francisco installed speed cameras at high-risk locations to reduce dangerous driving. Early reports say that speeding dropped sharply at camera locations after the program began. This is a positive statistics story because data are being used to evaluate whether a public policy changes behavior. It is also a careful-reasoning story: before we conclude that cameras caused safer streets, we need to ask how speeding was measured, what was compared, whether other things changed at the same time, and whether fewer speeding events led to fewer crashes or injuries.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

This story focuses on San Francisco's use of speed cameras at high-risk locations. Early reports describe large drops in speeding after cameras were installed, along with continuing variation across locations. The policy question is concrete: did the cameras change driver behavior, and what evidence would show whether streets became safer?

The story fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* because students can define cases and variables in several reasonable ways: vehicles, camera locations, days, street segments, speeding events, or crashes. It supports descriptive statistics, counts versus rates, percent change, before/after comparisons, practical significance, measurement quality, and cautious causal language. The Chronicle noted an important measurement detail: one evaluation used road tubes to measure speeding, not only camera citations, because citation data can be affected by outages, traffic patterns, events, and other complications.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: vehicles passing a camera site, camera locations, days, or time intervals.
- Variables: speed, speed limit, violation status, location, time of day, citation count, crash count.
- Counts versus rates: number of violations versus violations per vehicle.
- Before/after comparison.
- Practical significance: a large percent reduction may matter for public safety.
- Confounding: construction, traffic volume, weather, enforcement publicity, or road design.
- Measurement: road tubes, camera citations, and crash records each capture different information.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Define The Dataset

Ask students: If you were building the dataset, what is one row? A car trip, a camera, a day, or a street segment? Have groups choose a unit of analysis and list variables.

### Percent Change Practice

Give students a simple before/after table for three camera locations. They calculate percent change in speeding and decide which location changed the most in absolute and relative terms.

### Measurement Comparison

Compare two data sources: camera citations and independent road-tube measurements. Students list what each source can measure well and what could make each misleading.

### Causal Claim Ladder

Students place statements on a ladder from weakest to strongest evidence: "speeding was lower after cameras," "cameras may have contributed," "cameras caused the decline," and "cameras reduced injuries." They explain what extra evidence is needed to move up the ladder.

## Discussion Questions

1. What should count as a "case" in this story?
2. Is the main outcome a count, a proportion, a rate, or an average?
3. Why might citation data and road-tube data tell different stories?
4. What comparison would make the causal claim stronger?
5. If speeding decreases but crashes do not, did the policy work?
6. What graph would best show whether behavior changed over time?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Calculation

At one camera location, 2,000 speeding events were recorded before cameras and 500 after cameras. Calculate the absolute decrease and percent decrease. Then write one sentence explaining why this calculation alone does not prove the cameras caused the change.

### Graph Choice Question

Choose a graph for showing monthly speeding rates at camera locations before and after installation. Explain why your graph is appropriate and name one feature the graph should include.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: count versus rate.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: If you divide the number of speeding violations by the number of cars passing a site, what kind of quantity have you created?
- Answer: A rate or proportion.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: before/after limits.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: A drop in speeding after cameras were installed automatically proves the cameras caused the drop.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: percent decrease.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: At one site, speeding events dropped from 2,000 before cameras to 500 after cameras. What is the percent decrease?
- Answer: The decrease is 1,500. The percent decrease is 1,500/2,000 = 75%.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: interpreting measurement sources.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Why might road-tube speed measurements be more informative than citation counts alone when evaluating the program?
- Answer: Citation counts can be affected by outages, enforcement settings, or traffic patterns, while direct speed measurements better reflect actual driving behavior.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: outcome definition.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: If speeding decreases but crashes do not, why might people disagree about whether the policy "worked"?
- Answer: Different people may prioritize different outcomes, so a policy can succeed on behavior change without clearly succeeding on safety outcomes.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: stronger causal design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Propose one stronger comparison that would help evaluate whether speed cameras caused the change in driver behavior.
- Answer: A strong answer uses similar untreated locations, longer time series, or both, so that changes at camera sites can be compared against a better baseline.
