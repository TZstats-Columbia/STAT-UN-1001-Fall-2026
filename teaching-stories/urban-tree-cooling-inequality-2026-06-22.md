## Student-Facing Paragraph

A recent news story reports on a study of nearly 9,000 large cities around the world and says that urban trees offset about half of the extra heat cities create compared with nearby rural areas. But the cooling is not spread evenly: hotter and poorer cities often get much less benefit because they have less tree cover. This is a strong statistics story because it turns a familiar idea, "trees cool cities," into measurable quantities, while also raising questions about how averages can hide inequality across places.

## Synopsis And Course Fit

The story summarizes a global study of city temperatures, tree cover, and urban heat. The headline claim is appealingly simple: on average, trees cool cities. But the article becomes more statistically interesting when it shows that the average hides substantial variation. Some cities get meaningful cooling, while others get almost none. The comparison between wealthier and poorer cities also invites students to think carefully about confounding, background differences, and whether an observed pattern is purely about trees or also about climate, land use, water availability, and urban form.

This fits *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* very well because students can practice identifying cases, variables, and comparison groups; interpreting averages and variability; and distinguishing a descriptive association from a causal claim. It also works nicely for discussing ecological data, since the units are cities or city neighborhoods rather than individual people.

## *Introduction to Statistical Reasoning* Alignment

- Week 2: Discuss how measurement choices affect what is being compared across cities.
- Week 3: Interpret averages, spread, and subgroup comparisons.

## Statistical Concepts This Story Illuminates

- Cases: cities, neighborhoods, or city segments depending on the level of analysis.
- Variables: air temperature, tree cover, city size, city wealth, and climate zone.
- Average versus variation.
- Group comparison.
- Ecological data and unit of analysis.
- Association versus causation.
- Confounding and omitted variables.
- External validity across different kinds of cities.

## Suggestions For Class Activities

### Average Versus Spread

Show students the average cooling effect and ask whether that is enough to describe the full story. Then ask what additional summaries or graphs they would want to see.

### Cases And Variables

Ask students to name the cases and variables if the dataset is organized by city, and then again if it is organized by neighborhood. Use this to reinforce that the definition of a case depends on the dataset.

### Causal Language Check

Provide statements such as "Planting trees solves urban heat" or "Poorer cities are hotter because they do not value trees." Have students revise them into more defensible statistical language.

### Graph Design

Ask students to sketch a graph comparing cooling effects in wealthier versus poorer cities. Then discuss what a graph can show clearly and what it still cannot establish.

## Discussion Questions

1. What is the main claim in this story?
2. What are the cases, and what are the key variables?
3. Why can a global average be useful but also misleading?
4. What other factors besides tree cover might affect city temperatures?
5. Why does a difference between richer and poorer cities not automatically prove a single cause?
6. What graph would best show both the average effect and the unequal distribution across cities?

## Example Assessment Instruments

### Short Answer

The article says trees offset about half of the extra heat cities create on average. Explain what "on average" means here and give one reason that average may not describe every city well.

### Cases And Variables Prompt

Identify the likely cases and at least three variables in this study. Then explain whether the study is about individuals or about larger units such as cities.

### Exit Ticket

Why is it statistically important that the cooling effect differs across cities instead of being the same everywhere?

### Mini-Rubric

- Correctly identifies the units being studied.
- Recognizes the difference between an average and variation around that average.
- Notes at least one possible confounder.
- Avoids overstating causation.

## Quiz Problems

### Quiz Problem 1

- Concept tested: unit of analysis.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: naming concepts.
- Prompt: In the main version of this story, what is the most natural unit of analysis: individual people or cities?
- Answer: Cities.

### Quiz Problem 2

- Concept tested: average versus spread.
- Difficulty: Easy.
- Task type: true/false.
- Prompt: True or false: If trees offset about half of extra urban heat on average, then every city gets about the same cooling benefit.
- Answer: False.

### Quiz Problem 3

- Concept tested: interpreting subgroup differences.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: calculation.
- Prompt: Suppose wealthier cities average 2.0 degrees of cooling and poorer cities average 0.8 degrees. What is the difference in average cooling?
- Answer: 1.2 degrees.

### Quiz Problem 4

- Concept tested: ecological interpretation.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: interpreting results.
- Prompt: Explain in one or two sentences why a global average cooling effect can hide important inequality across cities.
- Answer: Some cities may get much more cooling and others much less, so the average can mask large variation. Policy decisions may depend on that variation, not just the mean.

### Quiz Problem 5

- Concept tested: confounding.
- Difficulty: Medium.
- Task type: short answer.
- Prompt: Name one possible confounder when comparing cooling in wealthier versus poorer cities.
- Answer: A strong answer names a factor such as climate zone, water availability, urban density, or land-use pattern.

### Quiz Problem 6

- Concept tested: better comparison design.
- Difficulty: Challenging.
- Task type: design a study.
- Prompt: Describe one study design or analysis that would strengthen a claim that more tree cover contributes to cooler urban temperatures.
- Answer: A strong answer proposes comparing similar cities or neighborhoods while accounting for other factors, or using before/after data when tree cover changes.
