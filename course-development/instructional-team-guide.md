# STAT UN 1001 Instructional Team Guide

Fall 2026 draft

This guide consolidates instructional operations for the instructor and TAs: weekly rhythm, TA roles, flipped-activity analysis, grading, Gradescope, project support, AI policy support, and meeting structure.

## Course Identity

STAT UN 1001 is an introductory statistics course focused on statistical reasoning, intuition, and responsible data-based claims. The course should feel rigorous, welcoming, and practical.

The course is not mainly about memorizing formulas or becoming a programmer. Students should learn to ask better questions, interpret evidence carefully, and communicate uncertainty honestly.

## Semi-Flipped Classroom Rhythm

| When | Student Task | Instructional Team Task |
|---|---|---|
| Thursday | Next week's flipped activity is posted on Canvas | Finalize and test the activity |
| Friday-Monday | Students read, watch, try examples, and complete the flipped activity | Monitor questions as needed |
| Monday evening | Flipped activity due | Review completion and response patterns |
| Tuesday class | Students bring questions and practice concepts | Teach from student questions, misconceptions, and examples |
| Wednesday afternoon | No regular student deadline | Hold TA meeting and refine Thursday plan |
| Thursday class | Activity, project work, peer review, or quiz | Support class logistics and learning |
| Friday | Students see follow-up notes or grading progress | Grade, calibrate, summarize issues, prepare next announcement |

## Weekly TA Meeting

Default meeting time: Wednesday afternoon for 50-60 minutes.

Why Wednesday works:

- It comes after Tuesday class, so the meeting can respond to what students actually understood or missed.
- It comes before Thursday class, so there is still time to adjust an activity, quiz logistics, project workshop, or peer-review plan.
- It is close enough to Friday grading that TAs can leave with clear grading/calibration tasks.
- It protects Monday evening for flipped activity analysis rather than turning it into a rushed meeting.

Suggested agenda:

1. Student pulse, 5 minutes
   - Attendance, confusion, office-hour patterns, student concerns.

2. Flipped activity and Tuesday class evidence, 10 minutes
   - What students understood.
   - What they confused.
   - Which questions deserve class time.

3. Thursday plan, 15 minutes
   - Run through the activity, quiz, project workshop, or peer review.
   - Assign TA roles for class.

4. Grading and calibration, 15 minutes
   - Gradescope quiz grading, peer review rubrics, project rubrics, or regrade issues.

5. Next flipped activity, 10 minutes
   - Confirm it is ready to post by Thursday.
   - Flag wording, length, or accessibility issues.

6. Logistics, 5 minutes
   - Office hours, late-registering students, Canvas questions, accommodations, scanning.

## TA Roles

TAs will likely help with:

- Holding office hours.
- Answering Canvas discussion questions.
- Supporting in-class activities and project workshops.
- Grading quizzes, peer reviews, final projects, and exams.
- Helping scan, upload, and grade paper assessments in Gradescope.
- Helping maintain rubrics and grading consistency.
- Flagging common student misconceptions.
- Reviewing flipped activity responses for patterns, not detailed grading.
- Testing course materials, datasets, applets, retrieval results, and feedback-assistant responses.
- Supporting in-class use of the anonymous Data Search and Retrieval Assistant and Project Feedback Assistant without collecting student conversation histories.
- Supporting late-registering students as they catch up.

## Flipped Activity Analysis

Flipped activities are worth 10% of the course grade by completion. TAs do not need to grade the correctness of each response unless a particular activity says otherwise.

Instead, TAs should help analyze:

- Which questions had low completion or high confusion.
- Which terms or concepts students are misusing.
- Which student questions should shape Tuesday's class.
- Which examples or datasets students found interesting.
- Which issues should become a Thursday activity.
- Which students may need outreach because they are repeatedly missing completion work.

Useful output for the instructor is a short weekly note, not a long report. A good note might say: "Many students treated random assignment and random sampling as interchangeable. Several asked whether a biased sample can still be useful. Suggested Tuesday emphasis: population versus treatment assignment; Thursday activity: redesign a weak study."

## Quiz Week Flow

On most quiz weeks, Thursday may be used for the 20-minute quiz plus a shorter activity or debrief. Quiz 5 is the Thanksgiving-week exception and is scheduled for Tuesday, November 24.

Suggested quiz-day flow:

- First 10 minutes: settle, distribute quiz, clarify logistics only.
- Next 20 minutes: quiz.
- Next 10 minutes: collect and organize papers for scanning.
- Remaining time: brief application, project work, or reflection question.

After class:

- Scan quizzes.
- Upload to Gradescope.
- Confirm page/question assignment.
- Calibrate rubric with a small shared sample.
- Divide grading across TAs.
- Return grades with common-feedback summary.

## Grading Principles

Grade for statistical reasoning, not just final answers.

Reward students who:

- Define variables, populations, and context clearly.
- Distinguish association from causation.
- Interpret uncertainty in words.
- Use graphs and summaries appropriately.
- Explain limitations honestly.
- Check whether conclusions match the evidence.
- Acknowledge AI use transparently when relevant.

Do not reward unsupported certainty, even when the computation is correct.

## Quiz Grading

Quizzes are 20-minute paper assessments worth 20% of the course grade, with the lowest quiz score dropped. They use question formats similar to the cumulative final exam, but quiz questions should be more focused and less variable in difficulty. They will be scanned and graded in Gradescope by TAs.

Before grading:

- Review the answer key and rubric together.
- Identify acceptable alternative phrasings.
- Decide how to handle arithmetic mistakes separately from conceptual mistakes.
- Grade a small shared sample and compare scores before dividing the work.
- Use Gradescope rubric items consistently. If a new common answer pattern appears, pause and check before creating a rubric adjustment.

After grading:

- Record common errors.
- Suggest one or two issues for review in the next class.
- Identify questions that may have been ambiguous.
- Check that scans are assigned correctly and that regrade requests are handled according to course policy.

## Final Exam Grading

The cumulative final exam is worth 40% of the course grade. It will be completed on paper, scanned, and graded in Gradescope using shared rubrics. The final should feel familiar in format because of the quizzes, but it may include a wider range of difficulty and more synthesis across topics.

Final exam grading should use the same calibration process as quiz grading, but with extra care:

- Calibrate every multi-part problem before dividing work.
- Keep a running list of acceptable alternative explanations.
- Escalate ambiguous responses to the instructor.
- Watch for conceptual consistency across related parts.
- Use regrade request procedures consistently.

## Project and Peer Review Grading

Mini-project tasks are completed primarily during class and graded for completion, while peer reviews are graded using rubrics. The final project is worth 20% and should be graded using a rubric. Grade only the statistical artifact students intentionally submit; never request or evaluate assistant prompts, screenshots, conversation histories, or frequency of use.

Project rubrics should emphasize:

- Question clarity.
- Understanding of the dataset.
- Appropriate statistical summaries or methods.
- Interpretation and communication.
- Limitations and uncertainty.
- Reproducibility or transparency.
- Responsible AI acknowledgment, when applicable.

Project feedback should be specific enough that students know what to revise. Avoid vague comments like "be clearer" unless paired with a concrete suggestion.

Students may receive anonymous formative feedback from the Project Feedback Assistant as well as human feedback. TAs should help students interpret and evaluate feedback rather than treating AI feedback as authoritative. Students submit their revision, not the assistant conversation.

## Peer Review

TAs should help students learn that peer review is not just proofreading. Good peer review asks:

- What claim is being made?
- What evidence supports it?
- What is unclear?
- What alternative explanation should be considered?
- What limitation should be acknowledged?
- What would make the work more trustworthy?

## AI Policy Support

Students may use AI tools for brainstorming, coding help, revision, and feedback, but they must own the final work.

The course-provided Data Search and Retrieval Assistant and Project Feedback Assistant are anonymous and approved for mini-project work. The instructional team must not collect, retain, request, or evaluate student interaction histories. Remind students not to enter names, UNI identifiers, private information, or confidential data. Assistant use is not participation evidence and is not graded.

During in-class mini-project work, help students verify:

- Data source and provenance.
- Variable definitions and units.
- Geographic and temporal coverage.
- Missingness and representation.
- Whether the retrieved data actually answer the statistical question.
- Whether assistant feedback is statistically sound and appropriate for the course level.

Students may provide anonymous, ungraded feedback on retrieval failures, unclear responses, missing data, and possible improvements. Do not connect this feedback to grades or participation records.

When students ask whether a use is allowed, guide them with these questions:

- Did the AI replace your own reasoning, or support it?
- Can you explain every claim, calculation, and graph?
- Did you check factual claims, code, and citations?
- If you used an AI tool other than the two anonymous course assistants, did you acknowledge meaningful assistance?

TAs should not accuse students casually. If there is a concern about academic integrity, document the evidence and consult the instructor.

## AI-Aware Activity Facilitation

AI-aware activities should support introductory statistical reasoning. They should not become general debates about AI, machine learning, or technology policy unless those issues are directly tied to data, uncertainty, evidence, prediction, error, bias, or decision-making.

When facilitating an AI-related example, TAs should keep students anchored in statistical questions:

- What is being claimed?
- What outcome is measured, predicted, classified, or evaluated?
- What data were used?
- Who or what might be missing from the data?
- What population or setting does the claim apply to?
- What errors could occur?
- Are different errors equally serious?
- What decision might be made from the result?
- What additional evidence would be needed before trusting the claim?

If discussion drifts toward technical AI details, redirect to the relevant statistical concept: sampling, measurement, conditional probability, uncertainty, comparison, prediction error, generalization, or responsible communication.

## Supporting Students With Computation

The goal is to reduce friction without doing the work for students.

Good TA help:

- Ask the student to explain what they are trying to do.
- Help them read error messages.
- Point them to templates or examples.
- Explain what a command or output means.
- Ask them to interpret the result in context.

Avoid taking over the keyboard for long stretches or producing an analysis the student cannot explain.

## Late Registration Protocol

During the first three weeks, late-registering students should be directed to:

- The course welcome page.
- The Week 1-3 catch-up checklist.
- Any completion-based flipped activities still open for catch-up.
- Office hours or a designated TA catch-up session.
- The first project onboarding activity.

TAs should reassure students that the course was designed with a catch-up window, while also helping them make a concrete plan.

## Communication Norms

Use a warm, direct, and consistent tone.

- Answer public questions publicly when many students may benefit.
- Move private grade, health, or accommodation issues to private channels.
- Escalate policy questions to the instructor.
- Do not improvise grading exceptions without checking.
- Keep a record of recurring issues.
