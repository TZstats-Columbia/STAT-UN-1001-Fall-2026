# STAT UN 1001 Learning Objective Map

This document maps weekly learning objectives to flipped activities, Tuesday class, Thursday class, project arc activities, and assessment evidence.

## How to Read This

Use two levels of detail:

- The quick map gives a one-screen overview of what each week is trying to accomplish.
- The detailed weekly map shows how each objective is practiced and assessed.

Recommended review order:

1. Scan the quick map for pacing and coverage.
2. Read one week at a time in the detailed map.
3. Check whether each objective has at least one preparation activity, one in-class use, and one evidence source.

## Evidence Codes

- `FA`: flipped activity completion or response pattern.
- `TUE`: Tuesday discussion, Q&A, or worked example.
- `THU`: Thursday activity, workshop, peer review, or quiz.
- `PROJ`: mini-project, final project, or peer review.
- `QUIZ`: paper quiz.
- `FINAL`: cumulative final exam.

## Detailed Weekly Map

### Week 1: What Is Statistics?

Objective 1: Explain what statistics is as a discipline, what it is used for, and what statisticians do.

- Course entry survey: gathers diagnostic information about students' goals, prior experience, concerns, and future needs; it is not evidence of mastery.
- Tuesday: course orientation and the opening “Why Statistics?” discussion introduce the discipline and its uses.
- Evidence: `TUE`.

Objective 2: Explain why statistical reasoning matters.

- Tuesday: opening examples connect statistical reasoning to learning from data.
- Thursday: the Claim vs Data activity asks students to distinguish claims from supporting information.
- Evidence: `TUE`, `THU`.

Objective 3: Identify cases, variables, values, populations, samples, and claims.

- Tuesday: course examples introduce and apply the core vocabulary.
- Thursday: the Claim vs Data activity reinforces the vocabulary in context.
- Project arc: the concepts prepare students for the semester project sequence.
- Evidence: `TUE`, `THU`, `PROJ`.

Objective 4: Distinguish a data-based claim from the evidence needed to support it.

- Tuesday: students consider how cases, variables, populations, and samples relate to claims.
- Thursday: the Claim vs Data activity asks what evidence a claim would require.
- Project arc: introduction to the semester project sequence connects claims to later data investigations.
- Evidence: `TUE`, `THU`, `PROJ`.

### Week 2: Study Design

Objective 1: Distinguish observational studies, experiments, surveys, and anecdotes.

- Flipped: OpenIntro-based questions and the urban-rat study ask students to distinguish observational studies from experiments.
- Tuesday: review scenario patterns and student confusions.
- Thursday: redesign a weak study.
- Project arc: Mini-project 0 begins with an in-class question and retrieval trial; Mini-project 1 data biography begins.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `PROJ`.

Objective 2: Explain random sampling and random assignment in plain language.

- Flipped: the family-size and urban-rat questions diagnose sampling and generalization; the vitamin-C and chia-seed questions diagnose treatment assignment and experimental design.
- Tuesday: targeted contrast between population representation and causal comparison.
- Thursday: redesign activity requires choosing sampling and/or assignment strategy.
- Project arc: data biography and retrieval audit asks how observations were obtained and verifies provenance, coverage, units, missingness, and representation.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `FINAL`.

Objective 3: Identify bias, confounding, and limits of causal claims.

- Flipped: students identify size-biased household sampling, reporting-system bias in rat complaints, and limits on generalization from volunteers or nonrandom city samples.
- Tuesday: algorithmic or AI claim used to surface confounding and measurement limits.
- Thursday: students explain what improved design still cannot prove.
- Project arc: data biography includes missingness and representation.
- Evidence: `FA`, `THU`, `PROJ`, `QUIZ`.

Objective 4: Connect study design to credibility of claims.

- Flipped: immediate answer feedback connects design features to association, causality, and generalizability; the anonymous check-in identifies concepts needing class review.
- Tuesday: discussion links design choices to strength of evidence.
- Thursday: redesign activity ends with a revised claim.
- Project arc: students decide what claims their chosen dataset can support.
- Evidence: `FA`, `THU`, `PROJ`, `FINAL`.

### Week 3: Describing Data

Objective 1: Classify variables as categorical or numerical.

- Flipped: Chapter 2 practice uses numerical distributions, binary indicators, and two-way categorical tables; automatic feedback reinforces the role of variable type in choosing a summary.
- Tuesday: rapid retrieval distinguishes categorical and numerical variables, then connects variable type to indicators, frequency tables, histograms, and two-way tables.
- Thursday: students apply variable classification while refining project questions and identifying variables in retrieved data; no new Chapter 2 content is introduced.
- Project arc: students label variables in a candidate dataset.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `QUIZ`.

Objective 2: Choose appropriate summaries and visualizations.

- Flipped: students construct binned counts and a contingency table, match histograms to box plots, interpret three bar-plot forms modeled on Figure 2.23, and explain when proportions are more informative than counts.
- Tuesday: build raw values into binned counts and a histogram; compare mean/median and count/conditional-proportion choices; and contrast count, side-by-side, standardized, and mosaic displays.
- Thursday: students apply summary and display choices during project consultations and peer critique.
- Project arc: Mini-project 0 is completed by identifying the question, retrieved data, cases, variables, population, and limitations.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `PROJ`, `FINAL`.

Objective 3: Interpret distributions using center, variability, shape, and unusual observations.

- Flipped: students compute a mean, median, and quartiles; interpret modes and skew; compare mean/SD and median/IQR; and compare two nongendered transit-route distributions using center and spread.
- Tuesday: worked examples cover mean notation, 0/1 indicators, quartiles and IQR, variance and standard deviation, skew, transformations, and full distribution comparisons.
- Thursday: students use descriptive language only as needed to clarify a project question or retrieved dataset; no new Chapter 2 content is introduced.
- Project arc: descriptive language becomes part of exploratory story.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `QUIZ`.

Objective 4: Explain how summaries can reveal or hide information.

- Flipped: students identify curvature hidden by a straight-line description, explain what a log scale reveals, distinguish row from column proportions, compare alternative summaries, and report which categorical display is easiest or hardest to interpret.
- Tuesday: discuss how bin rules, summary choice, transformations, conditioning, and alternative displays can reveal or hide patterns in the same data.
- Thursday: project discussion and peer critique emphasize whether the proposed claim matches the variables, data source, and study design.
- Project arc: students note one limitation of a summary.
- Evidence: `FA`, `TUE`, `THU`, `PROJ`, `FINAL`.

## Readability Notes

To keep this easy to use:

- Keep this map separate from the detailed weekly content plan.
- Use objective numbers only within each week; avoid a large cross-semester numbering system until the course stabilizes.
- Use evidence codes instead of long grading descriptions.
- When building Canvas pages, copy only the relevant week's objectives into that week's module.
- Before finalizing quizzes and the final exam, check that each major objective appears in at least one quiz, project task, or final exam pathway.
